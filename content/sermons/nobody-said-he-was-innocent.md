Picture a boy sent out of class to the office. He knows what he did, and the person who caught him is right there with the list.

Now picture the teacher never looking at the list. She looks at his ruined shirt and says, "Get him a clean one."

That is Zechariah chapter 3. The boy really is guilty, and nobody pretends otherwise.

## Where this happens

Zechariah was a prophet — someone who delivered messages from God to a nation (Zechariah 1:1). He spoke at a low point in Israel's history.

Israel had lost a war badly. Babylon burned their capital city, Jerusalem, and marched the survivors off to a foreign country. Years later a new empire let some go home, and they came back to rubble. The temple — the one building where the nation met God and brought its offerings — lay flat.

What Zechariah is shown next is not a building site. It is a courtroom.

The man on trial is Joshua, the high priest (Haggai 1:1) — the one man allowed into the temple's most sacred room, once a year, carrying blood from a killed animal, to ask God to forgive the nation. If anyone had to be clean, it was him.

He is not clean.

## 1. The charges were true, and God never denied them

> Then he showed me Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the Lord, and Satan standing at his right hand to accuse him.
>
> *Zechariah 3:1*

In Hebrew, the language this was written in, the word here is `ha-satan` — *the accuser*. It is a job title before it is a name, and the job is prosecution. The right hand was where a prosecutor stood in court.

Then we learn what the priest is wearing.

> Now Joshua was standing before the angel, clothed with filthy garments.
>
> *Zechariah 3:3*

The Hebrew word for filthy is `tso'im`. It is not the polite word for dusty; it comes from the word for human waste. Joshua is covered in the worst thing a person can be covered in, in the holiest place he knows. The accuser has a case.

Watch what God says back.

> The Lord rebuke you, O Satan. The Lord who has chosen Jerusalem rebuke you. Is not this a brand plucked from the fire?
>
> *Zechariah 3:2*

Read it for what is missing. God tells the accuser to be quiet, but never tells him he is wrong. A *brand plucked from the fire* is a half-burnt stick someone reached in and pulled out. It means: yes, he is scorched, and he is mine because I pulled him out.

That job did not end with Joshua.

> The accuser of our brothers has been thrown down, who accuses them day and night before our God.
>
> *Revelation 12:10*

John wrote that to Christians under pressure, and *day and night* is the detail. Accusation is not an event, it is a shift with no end — the rhythm of lying awake at two in the morning replaying what you said.

So if the charges are true, how does anyone survive? A writer in the Psalms, Israel's hymn book, asks exactly that:

> If you, O Lord, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand? But with you there is forgiveness, that you may be feared.
>
> *Psalm 130:3-4*

*Mark iniquities* means keep a running tab of wrongs. Nobody would still be standing. What makes God awesome is not that he audits well; it is that he forgives.

There is no back door either — "if we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves" (1 John 1:8). The way out is not proving the list wrong. Joshua never speaks in his own defence in the whole chapter.

## 2. God did not clean the clothes — he took them off

> Remove the filthy garments from him. Behold, I have taken your iniquity away from you, and I will clothe you with pure vestments.
>
> *Zechariah 3:4*

Notice the order. The clothes come off first, then God says what they meant: the filth was guilt, worn where everyone could see it. Servants strip him, God supplies the new clothes, and Joshua just stands there.

Jesus told a story that lands in the same place. A son takes his father's money, wastes it, and comes home filthy with a speech ready about how he no longer deserves to be called a son. The father cuts him off:

> But the father said to his servants, "Bring quickly the best robe, and put it on him."
>
> *Luke 15:22*

No lecture, no probation — clothes. Jesus told that to people who assumed God graded on effort, and the father never mentions the money.

Here is the fair objection. Is this a cover-up? Joshua walks out clean, but he is the same man who walked in. Paul answers it:

> For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.
>
> *2 Corinthians 5:21*

*Righteousness* is a heavy word for a simple thing: being in the clear. Two people trade places — Jesus, with nothing on his record, takes the filth, and the guilty person gets his standing. So God can silence the accuser without disputing one item on the list. The list was moved, not shredded.

You may still think you would turn up in better clothes than Joshua.

> All our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment.
>
> *Isaiah 64:6*

Isaiah does not mean our failures. He means our *good* deeds, the ones we would put on the table. Even the clean shirt goes in the pile — which is why nobody has to bring one. Being joined to Jesus, Paul says, is like putting him on (Galatians 3:27).

## 3. Clean clothes come with a job

The chapter does not end at the wardrobe. God speaks again, and this time there is an *if*.

> If you will walk in my ways and keep my charge, then you shall rule my house and have charge of my courts.
>
> *Zechariah 3:7*

The *if* is not about getting the clothes — Joshua already has them. It is about the work that follows. Forgiveness was free; being useful is a choice he now gets to make.

Paul puts both halves in one breath:

> For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works.
>
> *Ephesians 2:8-10*

*Grace* means a gift given to someone who has not earned it and cannot pay it back. The rescue is a gift, full stop — and in the next breath, we were made for work. Not work to get in; work because we are in. Grace also *trains* us to say no to what we know is wrong (Titus 2:11-12).

Then a line that seems to contradict it:

> Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you.
>
> *Philippians 2:12-13*

*Salvation* is the whole rescue — the stick pulled out of the fire and kept. Half of that verse sounds like earning it; the other half takes it back. Paul tells a church to take the whole thing seriously, then says the effort itself is God working inside them. You push, and the strength you push with was handed to you.

## 4. One Branch, one day

The courtroom ends with a promise that is not about Joshua at all.

> Behold, I will bring my servant the Branch... and I will remove the iniquity of this land in a single day.
>
> *Zechariah 3:8-9*

*Branch* is the Hebrew word `tsemach` — a green shoot pushing up out of the ground. Israel's royal family had been cut down like a tree, and a shoot is what comes out of a stump nobody expects anything from. Isaiah drew the same picture: a shoot from the stump of Jesse (Isaiah 11:1).

Jeremiah, a prophet who watched the city fall, names the Branch:

> I will raise up for David a righteous Branch... and this is the name by which he will be called: "The Lord is our righteousness."
>
> *Jeremiah 23:5-6*

That is the clothes again, turned into a name. Not *the Lord is righteous*, which everyone already believed, but *the Lord is our righteousness* — his rightness counted as ours.

Then the phrase that should stop you: *in a single day*. Not gradually, and not once a year forever, which is what Joshua's job actually was.

> Every priest stands daily at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins. But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, he sat down.
>
> *Hebrews 10:11-12*

Priests stood because they were never finished; there were no chairs in that building. Jesus sat down, which is what you do when the work is done.

Zechariah leaves one thread hanging, and it is honest of him:

> In that day every one of you will invite his neighbour to come under his vine and under his fig tree.
>
> *Zechariah 3:10*

A vine and a fig tree meant your own land, your own food, and enough peace to sit outside with someone. That day has not fully come. Zechariah's people went back to a half-built temple, and the world still has wars in it. The forgiveness landed on one day. The peace is still arriving.

## What to do with this

**Stop defending yourself to God.** When the two-in-the-morning list starts and the items on it are true, arguing will lose. Say the worst one out loud to God in plain words, then stop talking about it.

**Stop doing the accuser's job.** His work is reading out true things about people. If you keep a private list of what a brother, a classmate, or a parent got wrong, you are running that shift for free. This week, say one true good thing about that person, to their face.

**Do the work you were dressed for.** Pick the one thing that is plainly yours and that you have been avoiding — the phone call, the chore nobody assigned you — and do it before Sunday. Not to get the clothes. Because you are already wearing them.
