Two men walked into the same building to pray. One was the sort of person every
parent hopes their child becomes. The other was the sort you cross the street to
avoid.

You already know which one comes out well. That is the trouble: we skim the
middle, where one word sits that almost nobody has explained.

Jesus was a Jewish teacher who lived about two thousand years ago, and Christians
believe he was God's own Son. He taught in parables — short made-up stories, each
making one point. Luke, who wrote his life down, names this story's audience in
one line.

> He also told this parable to some who trusted in themselves that they were
> righteous, and treated others with contempt.
>
> *Luke 18:9*

Luke means people who were sure they were good and looked down on everyone else.
Both halves come back later.

The building is the temple in Jerusalem, the one place in the country where
animals were killed as an offering for sin. Not a quiet chapel: smoke, blood,
priests, crowds.

## 1. The religious man's prayer was completely true

The first man is a Pharisee — one of the strict religious teachers, respected by
everyone, serious about obeying God's law.

> God, I thank you that I am not like other men, extortioners, unjust,
> adulterers, or even like this tax collector. I fast twice a week; I give tithes
> of all that I get.
>
> *Luke 18:11-12*

He is not lying. God's law set aside one day a year for the nation's sin and told
Israel to go without food on it (*Leviticus 16:29*). He fasts twice a week. A
tithe means handing back a tenth of what you earn; he gives a tenth of
everything, and Pharisees counted even garden herbs (*Luke 11:42*). He really is
more careful than almost anyone reading this.

So what is wrong with him? Isaiah, a prophet who spoke for God, answered that
centuries earlier.

> All our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment.
>
> *Isaiah 64:6*

Notice what he measures. Not our worst days — our best ones. Set beside God, the
good material is the dirty laundry. Effort is not bad; it is just not what makes
a person right with God. Paul, once a Pharisee himself, later called his own
spotless record a total loss next to knowing Christ (*Philippians 3:7-8*).

But this is not "so being careful does not matter." Jesus blocks that exit.

> Unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will
> never enter the kingdom of heaven.
>
> *Matthew 5:20*

He does not tell them to aim lower than the scribes and Pharisees, the law
experts and the strict teachers. They are the most careful people in town, and he
says you need *more* than they have before you can belong to God's kingdom.
Impossible, if you must supply it. The second man shows where it comes from.

## 2. The second man did not ask for mercy. He asked for a sacrifice.

A tax collector collected money for Rome, the foreign empire occupying the land.
He worked for the people holding his neighbours down, and kept whatever extra he
could squeeze out. Picture a neighbour collecting your rent and handing it to an
invading army.

> But the tax collector, standing far off, would not even lift up his eyes to
> heaven, but beat his breast, saying, "God, be merciful to me, a sinner!"
>
> *Luke 18:13*

Most translations put "be merciful" there. That is not the ordinary Greek word
for mercy. Twenty-five verses later a blind man shouts, "Son of David, have mercy
on me" (*Luke 18:38*) — the common word, `eleēson`. Luke uses it elsewhere, and
not here.

The word here is `hilasthēti`. It belongs to the family of words for making
atonement — covering sin with a sacrifice so a guilty person can come near God
and live. He is not asking for a better mood. He is standing in the building
where sin was covered by blood, asking God to let a sacrifice cover his.

Once a year the high priest — the one man allowed into the temple's innermost
room — went in carrying blood.

> Then he shall kill the goat of the sin offering that is for the people and
> bring its blood inside the curtain… Thus he shall make atonement for the Holy
> Place.
>
> *Leviticus 16:15-16*

The blood went onto the lid of the gold chest that held God's law. The old Greek
version of the Jewish scriptures calls that lid the `hilastērion` — the same root
as the tax collector's word.

He also does not quite say "a sinner." In Greek he says *the* sinner. He has
stopped measuring himself against anyone. In that crowded temple there is one
sinner, and it is him.

But the temple never settled the matter. The blood came back every year — its own
admission that the problem was still there. Then it was settled for good.

> God put [Christ Jesus] forward as a propitiation by his blood, to be received
> by faith.
>
> *Romans 3:25*

"Propitiation" is a heavy old word. The Greek behind it is `hilastērion`, the
same word used for that lid in *Hebrews 9:5*. Whether Paul means the lid itself
or the wider act it stood for, the word now lands on a person: Jesus is where the
blood goes. The tax collector asked for something he could not see, and God
answered in public, on a cross.

## 3. He went home right with God before he had fixed anything

Jesus gives the verdict himself.

> I tell you, this man went down to his house justified, rather than the other.
>
> *Luke 18:14*

"Justified" is a courtroom word. It means declared right — a judge's ruling, not
a report card showing improvement. A defendant hears it. A student cannot earn
it.

Count what he has done to deserve it. He has not repaid anyone, resigned, or
promised anything, and Jesus says he is right with God. That should be
uncomfortable.

Paul says it in a sentence that sounds almost illegal.

> To the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his
> faith is counted as righteousness.
>
> *Romans 4:5*

Look at the word "ungodly." Paul does not say God declares good people good; that
would not be news. He says God declares *guilty* people right — which no judge
may do unless someone else has already taken the penalty. Someone has. A gift to
people who cannot repay it is what Christians mean by grace, and Paul says God
set it up so nobody could boast (*Ephesians 2:8-9*).

Now the honest difficulty. James, another writer in the same Bible, says:

> A person is justified by works and not by faith alone.
>
> *James 2:24*

That looks like a flat contradiction, and pretending otherwise would be
dishonest. They answer different questions. Paul asks what gets a guilty person
declared right. James asks how you tell a living faith from a dead one, and
answers: watch what it does. One chapter later, Luke shows another tax collector
giving half his money to the poor and repaying everyone he cheated four times
over (*Luke 19:8*). Nobody set that as the entry price. It is what a forgiven man
does on his way out.

## 4. Being sure you are good and looking down on people are one illness

Go back to Luke's opening line. Jesus aimed this at people who "trusted in
themselves that they were righteous, and treated others with contempt"
(*Luke 18:9*). Two symptoms, one disease.

Watch it inside the prayer. The Pharisee cannot say who he is without saying who
he is not: "not like other men… or even like this tax collector." Remove the
other man and he has nothing left to give thanks for.

Jesus explained why at a dinner, when a Pharisee was disgusted that a woman with
a bad reputation was touching Jesus' feet. He told a short story about two
debtors who were both let off, then asked which one would love the lender more.

> He who is forgiven little, loves little.
>
> *Luke 7:47*

Your love runs on the size of the debt you know you owed. Shrink the debt in your
mind and the love shrinks with it. That is arithmetic, not a call to try harder
to feel warm.

Paul asks it as a question.

> What do you have that you did not receive? If then you received it, why do you
> boast as if you did not?
>
> *1 Corinthians 4:7*

Name one thing about yourself you made out of nothing. Your mind, your health,
the person who taught you to read — it all arrived. You can be grateful for a
gift or proud of it, but not both.

Paul never grew out of this. Decades later, writing to a younger man he had
trained, he says:

> Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost.
>
> *1 Timothy 1:15*

Not "I was." *I am.* The man who wrote many of the letters in the Bible still
described himself the way the tax collector did: the sinner, front of the queue.
It is the only address where grace can reach you.

## What to do with this

**Pray the tax collector's prayer this week, out loud, with nobody else in it.**
No names, no comparisons. Just: God, have mercy on me, the sinner.

**Catch the quiet comparison.** Most of us never boast out loud. We do it
silently: *at least I am not as bad as he is.* Catch one this week and stop it
mid-thought. Then say the harder thing: I do not know what he is carrying, and my
own account is not clear.

**Do the repaying, but keep the order right.** If you have taken something —
money, credit for someone else's work, an hour you were paid for — go and put it
right. Be clear about why: you are not buying the verdict, you already have it.

Two men walked into the same building. One brought a list. The other brought an
honest name for himself and one request: let the sacrifice count for me. One went
home as he came in. The other went home right with God, the bill already in the
hands of the man telling the story.
