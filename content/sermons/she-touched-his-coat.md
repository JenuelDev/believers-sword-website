A woman had spent every coin she owned on doctors and was worse than when she
started. Twelve years of that. Now she was pushing through a crowd with her head
down, aiming for the back of one man's coat.

She had a good reason to hide, and it was not shyness. The strange part of this
story is not that she got better, but which way the healing went.

Mark wrote one of the four accounts of Jesus' life that Christians keep. This
happens in Galilee, in northern Israel, about two thousand years ago. Jesus
was a Jewish teacher whom Christians believe is God's own Son.

He has just stepped off a boat when a man named Jairus falls at his feet. Jairus
runs the local synagogue — the meeting house where Jewish neighbours
gathered to read the Scriptures and pray — and his twelve-year-old daughter is
dying. Jesus goes with him, and the crowd follows.

Then someone with no name in the story gets in the way.

## 1. Twelve years of paying to get worse

> And there was a woman who had had a discharge of blood for twelve years, and
> who had suffered much under many physicians, and had spent all that she had,
> and was no better but rather grew worse.
>
> *Mark 5:25-26*

She had a bleeding illness that would not stop, and every doctor cost money. Mark
reports two things a kinder writer would soften: the money ran out, and the
treatments made her worse. Twelve years is also the exact age of the dying girl
Jesus is walking toward (*Mark 5:42*). This woman had been ill that girl's whole
life.

Centuries earlier, God had a prophet named Jeremiah — a man whose job was to say
God's words out loud — describe a wound nobody could close.

> Your hurt is incurable, and your wound is grievous… no medicine for your wound,
> no healing for you.
>
> *Jeremiah 30:12-13*

That is God refusing to pretend: nothing they could buy would fix it. It sounds
like the end of hope until five verses on — "I will restore health to you, and
your wounds I will heal" (*Jeremiah 30:17*). A cure exists, just not for sale.

A letter in the Bible called Hebrews turns that into a test, about the animals
Israel killed year after year to cover the nation's sin.

> Otherwise, would they not have ceased to be offered, since the worshipers,
> having once been cleansed, would no longer have any consciousness of sins?
>
> *Hebrews 10:2*

If a treatment works, you stop needing it. A queue that forms again every year
proves nothing was finished. That is her twelve years — pay, hope, fail, start
again. Most of us have a smaller version of it.

## 2. She was not allowed to touch anyone

> She had heard the reports about Jesus and came up behind him in the crowd and
> touched his garment. For she said, "If I touch even his garments, I will be
> made well."
>
> *Mark 5:27-28*

His garment is his outer coat, the loose cloak worn over everything else. Watch
how she does it: from behind, in a crowd, silent. A plan for not getting caught.

Her illness made her what the Bible calls unclean. That does not mean dirty, and
it does not mean guilty. It meant off-limits under God's law — shut out of the
worship of God, and able to pass that same state to whatever you touched.

> If a woman has a discharge of blood for many days… all the days of the
> discharge she shall continue in uncleanness.
>
> *Leviticus 15:25*

Anyone who touched her bed or her seat had to wash and stay off-limits until
evening (*Leviticus 15:27*). Read that as a life, not a rule. For twelve years
nobody hugged her, no chair she sat on was safe for the next person, and she
could not go where God was worshipped. She had done nothing wrong — she was ill —
and the result was the same as punishment: alone. So she reaches for his coat,
planning to vanish.

By everything that crowd had been taught, the plan should have hurt him. A
prophet named Haggai once tested the priests, whose job was to rule on what
counted as clean.

> "If someone carries holy meat… and touches… any kind of food, does it become
> holy?" The priests answered, "No." … "If someone who is unclean by contact with
> a dead body touches any of these, does it become unclean?" … "It does become
> unclean."
>
> *Haggai 2:12-13*

Two answers that do not match. Holy — belonging to God — does not spread by
touch. Unclean does. Put a clean hand on a muddy one and you get two muddy hands.
The crowd knew what her touch would do: not lift her up, but pull him down.

## 3. The rule ran the other way

> And immediately the flow of blood dried up, and she felt in her body that she
> was healed of her disease.
>
> *Mark 5:29*

It goes the wrong direction. Nothing of hers moves into him. Something of his
moves into her, through cloth, in a crowd, without a word. Mark does not explain
it. He reports it, and lets you notice that the oldest rule in the room has just
been overruled.

He had shown this once already. Earlier in the same book, a man with a serious
skin disease came to Jesus — the law told such a man to tear his clothes, live
outside the town, and call out "Unclean, unclean" so people stayed clear
(*Leviticus 13:45-46*). Jesus "stretched out his hand and touched him" and said,
"I will; be clean" (*Mark 1:41*). The word alone would have healed him. What that
man had lost most was being touched, and Jesus gave it back before he gave back
the skin.

Then he does it at full size. At Jairus's house the girl has already died. Under
Israel's law a dead body was the strongest source of
uncleanness there was — "Whoever touches the dead body of any person shall be
unclean seven days" (*Numbers 19:11*).

> Taking her by the hand he said to her, "Talitha cumi," which means, "Little
> girl, I say to you, arise." And immediately the girl got up and began walking.
>
> *Mark 5:41-42*

*Talitha cumi* is Aramaic, the everyday language Jesus spoke at home; Mark keeps
his words, then translates. He takes the hand nobody else would take. Again,
death does not spread to him. Life spreads to her.

## 4. He stopped the crowd so she would not go home a secret

She could have slipped away. Jesus would not let her.

> And Jesus, perceiving in himself that power had gone out from him, immediately
> turned about in the crowd and said, "Who touched my garments?"
>
> *Mark 5:30*

To her, that must have sounded like being caught.

> But the woman… came in fear and trembling and fell down before him and told him
> the whole truth.
>
> *Mark 5:33*

The whole truth: twelve years, the money, the rule she just broke in front of
everyone. She says out loud the thing she came to avoid saying.

> Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace, and be healed of your
> disease.
>
> *Mark 5:34*

He does not call her *woman*. He calls her *daughter*, and it is the only time in
Mark's whole account that Jesus calls anyone that. She arrived as a problem to be
hidden and leaves as family, in front of the crowd that had kept its distance.

Then "made you well." The Greek word is `sesōken`, from `sōzō`, which covers both
being healed and being rescued; Mark uses it both ways. Her body was mended
before he said it. Something larger has been settled.

That is why he stopped everything. A quiet healing would have left her well and
still hiding. Isaiah described the servant God would send as gentle with what is
nearly broken — "a bruised reed he will not break" (*Isaiah 42:3*). Calling her
out was not exposure but that same gentleness, done in front of witnesses.

Now the honest difficulty, because this is not a technique. Paul, who wrote many
of the letters in the Bible, had a physical trouble he called a thorn.

> Three times I pleaded with the Lord about this, that it should leave me. But he
> said to me, "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in
> weakness."
>
> *2 Corinthians 12:8-9*

He asked, and the answer was no. Anyone who says enough faith always ends in a
cured body is not reading the whole Bible, and is setting sick people up to blame
themselves. What Jesus refuses is not every illness. It is leaving you
outside.

## Where the exchange finished

Israel's unclean lived outside the camp (*Leviticus 13:46*). That is where he
ended up.

> So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through
> his own blood.
>
> *Hebrews 13:12*

To sanctify is to make clean and set apart for God. He was executed outside the
city wall, the place kept for whatever had to be kept away, and he took the
position the woman had lived in for twelve years.

> For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might
> become the righteousness of God.
>
> *2 Corinthians 5:21*

There is the swap. He had nothing wrong with him and took what was wrong with us;
we had nothing right and were handed his standing before God. He
does not stand clear of the weight. He picks it up.

## What to do with this

**Name the thing you keep paying for.** Say it out loud to one person this week:
this has not worked, and I have stopped pretending it will.

**Tell one person the whole truth.** Her healing was real before she spoke;
speaking ended the hiding. Choose the person and the day this week.

**Come without cleaning up first.** If you are staying away from God until you
are more presentable, say it tonight in plain words: I am not fixed, and I am
coming anyway.

She reached for the back of his coat, hoping to take something unseen. She left
with the illness gone, the crowd listening, and a name she did not have that
morning. Daughter.
