A general can buy almost anything. Naaman had the king's ear, a war record,
silver, gold and a house full of servants. What he could not buy was clean skin.

He ran the army of Aram, the country north-east of Israel that most Bibles call
Syria. He also had the disease older translations call leprosy. The Hebrew word
covers a range of serious skin conditions, not only what doctors mean by it
today.

> Naaman... was a great man with his master and in high favour, because by him
> the LORD had given victory to Syria. He was a mighty man of valour, but he was
> a leper.
>
> *2 Kings 5:1*

Israel's God handed victories to Israel's enemy through this man, and the story
never explains itself. Then the verse turns on one word: *but*. In front of it is
a career. Behind it is what he thinks about at night.

## 1. The cure came from the person nobody asked

> Now the Syrians on one of their raids had carried off a little girl from the
> land of Israel, and she worked in the service of Naaman's wife. She said to her
> mistress, "Would that my lord were with the prophet who is in Samaria! He would
> cure him of his leprosy."
>
> *2 Kings 5:2-3*

A raid meant soldiers crossing the border to steal animals, goods and people.
This girl was taken by men under Naaman's command, and now she cleans his wife's
house. A prophet is someone who speaks for God; she means Elisha, and Samaria was
Israel's capital.

She could have said nothing and watched the disease finish him. Instead she says
where help is. Everything good in this chapter starts with one sentence from a
stolen child whose name the Bible never records.

That is a pattern, not an accident:

> God chose what is foolish in the world to shame the wise; God chose what is
> weak in the world to shame the strong.
>
> *1 Corinthians 1:27*

Paul wrote that to a church in Corinth, a port city where people cared a great
deal about who mattered. Look around your own church, he tells them: mostly
nobodies. God works through people the powerful would never pick, so that nobody
can take the credit.

Jesus brought this same story up in his home town of Nazareth, in the synagogue —
the hall where Jewish people met to read Scripture.

> And there were many lepers in Israel in the time of Elisha the prophet, and
> none of them was cleansed, but only Naaman the Syrian.
>
> *Luke 4:27*

He picked the detail his neighbours would hate: sick Israelites went unhealed,
and God cleaned up a foreign commander instead. The crowd tried to throw him off
a cliff (Luke 4:29). If mercy going to the enemy feels unfair, you have
understood it.

And the chapter never pays the girl back. She does not go home. Nobody thanks
her.

## 2. He had already decided how the healing should go

Naaman does this properly: a letter from his king, silver, gold, ten sets of
clothes, horses and chariots outside Elisha's house. Elisha does not come out. He
sends a servant to the door with one instruction — wash in the Jordan seven
times.

> But Naaman was angry and went away, saying, "Behold, I thought that he would
> surely come out to me... and wave his hand over the place and cure the leper."
>
> *2 Kings 5:11*

*I thought.* He had planned the whole scene on the journey: a ceremony, a hand
waved over the bad skin, himself in the middle. What arrived was a message at the
door, like a delivery.

He complains that the rivers of Damascus, his home city, beat anything in Israel
(2 Kings 5:12), and he is right. Those run clear off the mountains; the Jordan is
small, brown and muddy. His complaint is accurate and beside the point.

Isaiah wrote to Israelites who could not believe God would forgive so cheaply:

> Let him return to the LORD, that he may have compassion on him... for he will
> abundantly pardon. For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways
> my ways, declares the LORD.
>
> *Isaiah 55:7-8*

That last line gets quoted whenever something sad happens and nobody can explain
it. Read the sentence in front of it and you see the subject: God pardons more
generously than we would. His ways are higher in the direction of mercy. Naaman
wanted a cure that cost something impressive, and got one that cost only his
dignity.

Jesus gave the same sort of undignified instruction to a man born blind:

> He spat on the ground and made mud with the saliva. Then he anointed the man's
> eyes with the mud and said to him, "Go, wash in the pool of Siloam."
>
> *John 9:6-7*

Jesus spits in the dirt, puts mud on the man's face and sends him across the city
to rinse it off. He comes back able to see.

Paul admitted the offence never washes out:

> We preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles.
>
> *1 Corinthians 1:23*

A stumbling block is something you trip over; Gentiles were everyone who was not
Jewish. The centre of the Christian message is a man executed by the Romans on a
cross, with God calling that a rescue — an insult to some people and nonsense to
the rest. Take only the help that flatters you, and you go home with your
disease.

## 3. The cure made the great man small

His servants change his mind, the second time a servant saves him. Their argument
is simple: you would have done something hard and costly without blinking, so do
the easy thing (2 Kings 5:13).

> So he went down and dipped himself seven times in the Jordan, according to the
> word of the man of God, and his flesh was restored like the flesh of a little
> child, and he was clean.
>
> *2 Kings 5:14*

This book was first written in Hebrew, and the phrase there for "little child" is
*na'ar qaton*. In verse 2 the stolen servant was a *na'arah qetannah* — a little
girl. Same pair of words, one form for a boy and one for a girl. The story
describes an army commander with the phrase it used for the child slave he never
thought to ask. He walks into the water a general and comes out looking like her.

Jesus settled an argument about rank the same way:

> Truly, I say to you, unless you turn and become like children, you will never
> enter the kingdom of heaven.
>
> *Matthew 18:3*

His friends had just asked which of them was greatest. He stood a child in the
middle of them. A child then had no standing, no vote, nothing to trade. He
answers a question about rank by taking rank off the table.

Elisha healed Naaman from indoors, through a messenger. Jesus worked the other
way:

> Moved with pity, he stretched out his hand and touched him and said to him, "I
> will; be clean."
>
> *Mark 1:41*

A man with the same disease had begged him, "If you will, you can make me clean."
The first two words back answer the real question — *I will.* Then Jesus touches
him, which nobody did to people with that illness.

## 4. You cannot pay for it, and it is dangerous to try

Naaman comes back a different man: "I know that there is no God in all the earth
but in Israel" (2 Kings 5:15). Then he offers the silver and the gold.

> As the LORD lives, before whom I stand, I will receive none.
>
> *2 Kings 5:16*

Naaman presses; Elisha still refuses. That refusal is part of the cure. If the
prophet takes the money, the general rides home believing he bought his skin.

The same chapter of Isaiah opens with an invitation built the same way:

> Come, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters; and he who has no money, come,
> buy and eat!
>
> *Isaiah 55:1*

It is a market trader's shout with the price taken out: come and buy, and bring
nothing. The offer is aimed at people with empty pockets, which in the end is
everybody.

Paul says the whole rescue runs on that:

> For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing;
> it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast.
>
> *Ephesians 2:8-9*

Grace means a gift handed to someone who has not earned it and cannot pay for it.
Paul puts the reason at the end: *so that no one may boast.* A gift you helped
pay for is one you can bring up later.

Then the ending nobody expects. Elisha's servant Gehazi chases the convoy, lies,
and collects the silver his master turned down.

> Therefore the leprosy of Naaman shall cling to you... So he went out from his
> presence a leper, like snow.
>
> *2 Kings 5:27*

The disease changes address. The foreigner who took nothing goes home clean; the
insider who charged for God's mercy carries it out of the room.

## What to do with this

**Do the small plain thing.** Name the instruction you already have and keep
skipping because it looks too ordinary to matter — the apology, the phone call,
the money you said you would pay back. Do that one this week.

**Listen downwards.** Naaman was rescued twice by people who worked for him. When
someone with less standing than you says something you do not want to hear — a
younger brother or sister, the new person at work, your own child — let them
finish before you answer.

**Stop paying for a gift.** If you catch yourself thinking God likes you more
after a good week and less after a bad one, you are handing Elisha the silver.
Say the plain sentence back: it was a gift, and gifts do not come in instalments.

Naaman came for clean skin and went home with a confession he had not planned to
make. The girl who told him where to go never got her name in the book. And the
one thing this chapter will not let anybody do is pay.
