"The Lord will provide" is a comfortable sentence. It goes on mugs and fridge magnets.

The man who first said it was walking up a hill with a knife in his belt, and his son walked beside him carrying the firewood.

That line comes from Genesis 22. Almost nobody mentions this: what Abraham promised his son on the way up never turned up. Something else did.

## Who these people are

Abraham was an old man with no children. Years earlier God had promised him a family so large it would become a nation, and said every family on earth would be blessed through it (Genesis 12:2-3). He was a hundred when his son Isaac was finally born (Genesis 21:5). God tied the promise to this one boy, calling it a covenant with Isaac that would last forever (Genesis 17:19) — a covenant being a binding promise between two sides, closer to a contract than a wish.

Isaac is not just a much-loved child. He is the promise walking around in sandals, and everything God said rests on him staying alive.

Then God tells Abraham to take Isaac to a place called Moriah and offer him there as a burnt offering — an animal killed and burned on a stone platform as a gift to God. Killing an animal that way was ordinary then. Doing it to your child was never something the God of Abraham had asked.

Abraham gets up early, takes his son, and walks for three days.

## 1. The boy asked a fair question and got an answer his father could not back up

Somewhere near the top, Isaac works out that something is missing.

> Behold, the fire and the wood, but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?
>
> *Genesis 22:7*

He has seen this done before. You need fire, wood, and an animal, and he is carrying two of the three. He asks the way a child asks why there are no plates on the table.

His father answers:

> God will provide for himself the lamb for a burnt offering, my son.
>
> *Genesis 22:8*

Abraham has no lamb. He has not spotted one on a hillside. He is saying God will handle the missing part — while carrying a knife for the boy he says it to.

Where does a man get that? The letter to the Hebrews, written much later, says he had reasoned it out:

> He considered that God was able even to raise him from the dead.
>
> *Hebrews 11:19*

Abraham had two facts he could not fit together: the promise runs through Isaac, and God has said to give Isaac up. Both can only stand if God can undo death. So Abraham decided God could. That is a conclusion, not a mood.

Still, the Bible admits the difficulty. Micah, a prophet who spoke for God long after Abraham, imagined a man trying to buy God off:

> Shall I give my firstborn for my transgression, the fruit of my body for the sin of my soul?
>
> *Micah 6:7*

Micah's next line answers with a no: what God wants is to do justice, to love kindness, and to walk humbly with him (Micah 6:8). So if God does not want children, what is happening on that hill?

## 2. God stopped the knife, and the stopping is the message

The story does not end in a death. It ends in an interruption.

> Do not lay your hand on the boy or do anything to him, for now I know that you fear God, seeing you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me.
>
> *Genesis 22:12*

God stops it. Nobody dies. And he never asks anything like it again. Israel's law later forbids it and calls it one of the things God hates (Deuteronomy 12:31).

When Israel did it anyway, God's reaction is as strong as language gets:

> They have built the high places of Baal to burn their sons in the fire as burnt offerings to Baal, which I did not command or decree, nor did it come into my mind.
>
> *Jeremiah 19:5*

Baal was a local weather god, and this is what people believed he wanted. "Nor did it come into my mind" is the line to hold. God is not saying he allowed it reluctantly. The idea is not in him.

The test was never a request for a dead boy. It was a question: is God worth more to you than the gift God gave you? Abraham answered yes, and God took the knife out of the answer at once.

But Paul takes the same scene and turns it around:

> He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all.
>
> *Romans 8:32*

Paul is writing to Christians in Rome about how far they can trust God, and leans on this chapter. God told Abraham "you have not withheld your son" (Genesis 22:16). Paul says God did not spare his own. The knife stopped over Isaac. It did not stop over Jesus.

## 3. What arrived was not what he promised

Abraham looks up and sees an animal:

> And Abraham lifted up his eyes and looked, and behold, behind him was a ram, caught in a thicket by his horns.
>
> *Genesis 22:13*

Read that beside verse 8. It does not line up. The Hebrew word Abraham used for "lamb" is `seh` — a young sheep or goat, the small animal. The word here is `ayil` — a full-grown ram, with horns big enough to tangle in a bush. Isaac asked for a `seh`. Abraham promised a `seh`. What God sent was an `ayil`.

The ram is a real rescue, and Isaac walks home. But Abraham seems to know the promise is still unpaid, judging by what he names the place:

> So Abraham called the name of that place, "The Lord will provide"; as it is said to this day, "On the mount of the Lord it shall be provided."
>
> *Genesis 22:14*

The verb under "provide" is `ra'ah`, which usually means "to see" — close to our phrase *see to it*. Standing beside a dead ram and a living son, Abraham does not say the Lord provided. He says the Lord *will* provide. He has a rescue in hand and is still waiting on a lamb.

Generations later, Israel's king Solomon builds the temple, the one central building where sacrifices were made:

> Then Solomon began to build the house of the Lord in Jerusalem on Mount Moriah.
>
> *2 Chronicles 3:1*

Same hill. For roughly a thousand years lambs died on that ridge, day after day, in a building standing where a father was told to look for one.

And a thousand years of lambs did not settle the account:

> For it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins.
>
> *Hebrews 10:4*

That is written to people who grew up with the temple and assumed it was the answer. An animal cannot finally stand in for a person. The sacrifices were a signpost, not a destination. They taught one idea until people could recognise it when it walked in: substitution, someone else taking the hit coming to you.

Israel had already lived this. The night they left slavery in Egypt, every household killed a lamb and painted its blood on the doorframe:

> The blood shall be a sign for you, on the houses where you are. And when I see the blood, I will pass over you.
>
> *Exodus 12:13*

Notice the verb again — *when I see*. A lamb dies and a firstborn son lives. The same picture, at the size of a nation.

## 4. The lamb has a name

Two thousand years after Abraham, a preacher standing in a river watches a man walk toward him and says something nobody asked him to say:

> Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world.
>
> *John 1:29*

The preacher is John and the man is Jesus. Everyone listening knew what a lamb was for. John does not call him a teacher or a king. He calls him the animal you bring to be killed. "The world" is the size God named to Abraham at the start — not one family, everybody.

Isaiah had described this person seven hundred years earlier, with no name attached:

> Like a lamb that is led to the slaughter, and like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth.
>
> *Isaiah 53:7*

Isaiah is writing about a servant who suffers for other people's wrongdoing, and the detail he picks is the silence. Animals led to slaughter do not argue. Neither did Jesus at his trial.

In the last book of the Bible, John is shown God's throne, and what stands there is not a lion or a soldier:

> And between the throne and the four living creatures I saw a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain.
>
> *Revelation 5:6*

Standing, but still marked as killed. At the centre of everything is a lamb that was slaughtered and is somehow on its feet.

## What to do with this

**A late promise is not a broken one.** Abraham said God would provide the lamb, then watched a ram die instead. He was not wrong. He was early. If you asked God for something months ago, write the date down and keep it where you will see it — not as a countdown, but so you stop deciding every Friday that nothing is happening.

**Take the ram.** God gave Abraham a real rescue, just not the one he had named. If help has arrived from a direction you did not want — a job beneath you, an apology from someone you would rather stay angry at — take it, and thank whoever brought it.

**Stop trying to pay.** Micah asked whether God wants your firstborn, and the answer was no. He does not want your worst week as payment either. If you have been punishing yourself to pay for something you did, name it to God today and stop adding to the bill. It has been paid, and the one who paid does not need your help.

Isaac's question is still the right one. *Where is the lamb?* Abraham could only point forward. We can point at a person, and at a hill outside Jerusalem where a Father did not stop the knife.
