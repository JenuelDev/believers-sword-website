Most people know the fish: a man runs from God, a storm hits, sailors throw him
overboard, a huge fish swallows him, and three days later spits him out alive.

Almost nobody knows the ending. That same man sits on a hill outside a city in
the heat, angry enough to die, waiting to see whether it will be destroyed. God
asks him one question, and the book stops before he answers.

That ending is the point of the book. The fish is not.

Some background. Jonah was a prophet — someone God gave messages to say out loud
— in Israel, roughly 2,800 years ago. God told him to warn Nineveh, capital of
Assyria: the empire Israel feared most, and the one that would later destroy
northern Israel. Sending Jonah there was like sending a man to warn the army
marching on his town. He went the other way, boarding a ship for Tarshish,
far to the west. That is when the storm and the fish happen.

The second time God tells him, he goes, and preaches one line: *Yet forty days,
and Nineveh shall be overthrown* (*Jonah 3:4*). Five words in Hebrew, with no
mention of God by name and no offer of mercy. The city believes it anyway, right
up to the king, who gets off his throne and sits in ashes (*Jonah 3:6*). God does
not destroy them.

Then chapter 4 begins.

## 1. He ran because he knew what God is like

We assume Jonah ran because the job frightened him. He tells us himself, and that
is not the reason.

> That is why I made haste to flee to Tarshish; for I knew that you are a
> gracious God and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, and
> relenting from disaster.
>
> *Jonah 4:2*

He did not run because he was scared. He ran because he knew God would forgive
them. He is angry at God for being kind. *Gracious* means giving good things to
people who have not earned them. *Steadfast love* is the Hebrew word *hesed* —
loyalty that keeps a promise even after the other side has broken theirs.

Jonah did not invent that sentence. He is quoting:

> The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding
> in steadfast love and faithfulness.
>
> *Exodus 34:6*

Those are God's words about himself, spoken on a mountain to Moses, the man who
led Israel out of slavery in Egypt. They came right after the people made a gold
statue of a calf and worshipped it instead of God. That sentence became the line
Israel loved best, their proof that God had not given up. Jonah throws it back as
a complaint, because it turns out to cover somebody else too. What changed is not
God, but who else is getting in.

A different prophet wrote a whole book about this same city, and it opens with
the other half of the same sentence:

> The LORD is slow to anger and great in power, and the LORD will by no means
> clear the guilty.
>
> *Nahum 1:3*

Nahum wrote about Nineveh falling, and it did fall, well over a century after
Jonah stood in it. God spared a generation that turned around; he did not promise
the city could do as it liked forever.

## 2. The most successful sermon in the Bible left the preacher furious

The last verse of chapter 3 says God saw them turn from their evil way and spared
them (*Jonah 3:10*). The very next line:

> But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was angry.
>
> *Jonah 4:1*

More than a hundred thousand people change direction, and the preacher is not
glad. The Hebrew is blunt: it was *evil* to Jonah, a great evil — the same word
the book uses for Nineveh's wickedness. Its cruelty and its rescue get one label.

People stumble over God "relenting". Does he change his mind? Jeremiah, a later
prophet, says God stated the rule up front:

> If at any time I declare concerning a nation... that I will destroy it, and if
> that nation... turns from its evil, I will relent of the disaster that I
> intended to do to it.
>
> *Jeremiah 18:7-8*

That is a rule God gave in advance, not a change of mood. He warns of destruction
so people will turn, and if they turn it is no longer needed. A parent who says
"if you keep running you will fall" has not lied when the child slows down.

Centuries later Jesus described what happens in heaven when someone turns:

> There will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over
> ninety-nine righteous persons who need no repentance.
>
> *Luke 15:7*

To *repent* is to turn around and walk the other way, not just to feel bad. Heaven
throws a party over one person doing that; Jonah walks out and sulks. You can be
on God's side of an argument and on the wrong side of his joy.

## 3. God argued with him about a plant

Jonah goes east of the city, builds a shelter, and sits down to watch. God
appoints a plant that shades his head, and Jonah is *exceedingly glad* because of
it (*Jonah 4:6*). That is the only time in the book Jonah is happy. Not when
the city was spared. When he got shade.

The word *appointed* is worth noticing — the same verb used for the great fish
(*Jonah 1:17*). God appoints a fish, a plant, a worm, then a hot wind. This is not
punishment but a lesson, and every prop belongs to God. At dawn the worm
kills the plant, a scorching east wind comes up, and Jonah asks to die (*Jonah
4:7-8*). Then God makes his argument:

> You pity the plant, for which you did not labour, nor did you make it grow,
> which came into being in a night and perished in a night.
>
> *Jonah 4:10*

You did not plant it, water it, or wait for it, God says. It lasted a day, and you
were heartbroken when it died. So you are not a man without compassion, Jonah.
Your compassion works fine — it is just aimed at your own comfort.

Notice the order. Jonah prayed to die, and God's first move was to grow him
shade. He has done this before. Elijah, another prophet, ran into the desert in
fear for his life, sat down under a bush, and asked God to let him die. The
answer was not a talking-to:

> And behold, an angel touched him and said to him, "Arise and eat."
>
> *1 Kings 19:4-5*

Sleep and a hot meal came first. God is not shocked when an exhausted man says
something terrible.

It would be tidy to say Jonah's anger was the sin, but the Bible will not allow
it:

> And he looked around at them with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart.
>
> *Mark 3:5*

That is Jesus in a place of worship, about to heal a man with a shrivelled hand
while religious leaders watch to see if he breaks their rules. Mark says why he is
angry: hard hearts grieve him. Anger is not automatically wrong; the question is
what you are furious about. Jesus is angry that people are kept from help. Jonah
is angry that people are helped.

## 4. The book ends with a question, and it is aimed at you

Here is the last verse of Jonah. There is no chapter 5.

> And should not I pity Nineveh, that great city, in which there are more than
> 120,000 persons who do not know their right hand from their left, and also much
> cattle?
>
> *Jonah 4:11*

God does not defend himself.

"Do not know their right hand from their left" is not about directions. It means
they cannot tell right from wrong, because nobody ever taught them. God calls the
enemy city confused, not simply evil. Then he mentions the cows. Everything in
that city that breathes is on God's list. None of it was on Jonah's.

The book records no answer from Jonah. We never learn whether he softened or just
sat there. The question is left open because it is asked of whoever is reading.

Jesus told a story with the same shape. A younger son wastes his father's money
and comes home, and the father throws a party. The older son, who did everything
right, will not go in:

> But he was angry and refused to go in. His father came out and entreated
> him... "It was fitting to celebrate and be glad, for this your brother was
> dead, and is alive."
>
> *Luke 15:28-32*

That story also stops without an answer. Two stories, centuries apart, both ending
with a father outside in the dark, asking an angry man to come in and be glad
about someone he cannot stand.

Jesus also made it a command, and gave the reason:

> Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be sons
> of your Father who is in heaven. For he makes his sun rise on the evil and on
> the good, and sends rain on the just and on the unjust.
>
> *Matthew 5:44-45*

Look at the reason. Not "because your enemies deserve it" — they may not. Because
that is already how your Father treats them. Every field belonging to every cruel
person on earth grew under God's sun this year. Praying for an enemy is not
pretending they are good. It is wanting for them what God already wants.

## What to do with this

**Name your Nineveh.** Almost everyone has one — a person or group you would
quietly rather see fail. Say the actual name to God out loud this week. Not "help
me be nicer." The name.

**Then ask God for one good thing for them.** One sentence, out loud, every day
for a week. It will feel false at first. Do it anyway. Jonah had the
theology exactly right and prayed it as a complaint.

**Work out what your plant is.** Watch what genuinely upsets you this week — the
lost thing, the slow queue, the message nobody answered. That is where your
compassion points. God never told Jonah to care less about the plant, only to care
about the city as much.

The book has no ending because it is waiting for one. God asked his question
and stopped talking. Jonah is not recorded as answering. You are.
