There is a kind of tiredness that arrives after the win, not the loss. You finish
the long shift, or the months of caring for someone ill, and instead of feeling
good you sit down and cannot get up.

That happened to Elijah. He was a prophet — someone who spoke for God to a nation
that did not want to listen. He lived in Israel about eight hundred years before
Jesus, under King Ahab and Queen Jezebel, who had made the worship of a storm god
called Baal the official religion. Elijah challenged them in public. On a hill
called Carmel he built an altar — a stone table for burning an offering — and
asked God to settle the matter.

> Then the fire of the LORD fell and consumed the burnt offering... And all the
> people fell on their faces and said, "The LORD, he is God."
>
> *1 Kings 18:38-39*

A whole crowd changes its mind on the spot. Then Jezebel promises to have Elijah
killed, and he runs south, out of the country, into the desert.

## 1. He asked to die right after the biggest day of his life

> He came and sat down under a broom tree and asked that he might die: "It is
> enough; now, O LORD, take away my life, for I am no better than my fathers."
>
> *1 Kings 19:4*

A broom tree is a scrubby desert bush with barely enough shade for one person.
Elijah walks a day past the last town, sits under it, and asks God to end his
life. *I am no better than my fathers* means: I thought I would be different,
and I am not.

He never says God failed him. He watched the fire fall. What he cannot carry is
himself — and even at the bottom, he is still talking to God.

He was not the first. Moses led Israel out of slavery in Egypt and across a
desert, and partway through said this:

> I am not able to carry all this people alone; the burden is too heavy for me...
> kill me at once.
>
> *Numbers 11:14-15*

The same load, the same wish. Two of the most important men in Israel's history
said that to God, so it is not proof that a person's faith has collapsed.

Paul, who wrote many New Testament letters, had his own version of it:

> We were so utterly burdened beyond our strength that we despaired of life
> itself... But that was to make us rely not on ourselves but on God who raises
> the dead.
>
> *2 Corinthians 1:8-9*

Paul does not call it pleasant or deserved. He says it took something out of his
hands: he had been carrying the outcome, and he stopped. That is Elijah's
trouble too — he thinks a nation's future rests on him.

## 2. God's first answer was food and sleep, given twice

Elijah asks to die, then falls asleep. God's opening move is not a lecture. An
angel — a messenger sent by God — wakes him, and there is bread baked on hot
stones and a jar of water by his head. He eats and lies back down. So the
messenger comes again.

> Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you.
>
> *1 Kings 19:7*

God does not tell Elijah he is stronger than he thinks. God agrees with him. The
journey is too much for you, and that is the reason for the meal, not a rebuke.

Jesus later put the same care into an invitation:

> Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my
> yoke upon you... for I am gentle and lowly in heart.
>
> *Matthew 11:28-29*

A yoke is the wooden bar laid across two oxen so they pull a plough together. So
Jesus is not offering an end to work, but a shared harness with himself on the
other side.

But rest is not an escape hatch. The night before he was killed, Jesus prayed in a
garden to be spared what was coming.

> And there appeared to him an angel from heaven, strengthening him. And being in
> agony he prayed more earnestly.
>
> *Luke 22:43-44*

The same pattern — a messenger sent to a man at the end of himself. But it did not
remove what he dreaded. He was strengthened, and then he went. Elijah eats, then
walks forty days deeper into the desert. Being helped is not the same as being let
off.

## 3. God refused to be in the fire

Elijah walks to Horeb, the mountain also called Sinai, where God had given Israel
his laws centuries earlier. God's first words there are a question: "What are you
doing here, Elijah?" Elijah pours out his complaint — the people have abandoned
God, they killed the other prophets, and he alone is left. Then he is sent outside
to stand on the mountain.

> A great and strong wind tore the mountains, but the LORD was not in the
> wind... after the wind an earthquake, but the LORD was not in the earthquake...
> after the earthquake a fire, but the LORD was not in the fire. And after the
> fire the sound of a low whisper.
>
> *1 Kings 19:11-12*

Three huge events, each followed by a line saying God was not in it. Days before,
fire had fallen on Carmel and a nation shouted. Now God sends a fire and stays out
of it.

The Hebrew behind that last phrase is *qol demamah daqqah*: *qol* is a sound,
*demamah* is silence, *daqqah* means thin. A sound of thin silence. The odd word is
*demamah*, used for what happens to a storm once it is over:

> He made the storm be still, and the waves of the sea were hushed.
>
> *Psalm 107:29*

That song is about sailors who thought the sea would kill them, and the word for
the calm they got is the word in Elijah's verse. So what reached Elijah was not a
whisper of instructions but the hush after the noise stopped.

That is not a rule, though. Job, a man who lost his children and his health,
demanded an explanation, and God answered him out of a whirlwind (Job 38:1).
Horeb is narrower: God refused to repeat the display that had already worked,
because it was not what Elijah needed.

Here is the part usually left out. After the wind, the earthquake, the fire and
the thin silence, God asks the same question again — and Elijah gives the
identical answer, word for word. A powerful experience is rarely what repairs a
person.

## 4. The cure was a fact and a job

God never answers the complaint. He hands out instructions.

> Go, return on your way... anoint Hazael to be king over Syria, Jehu to be king
> over Israel, and Elisha the son of Shaphat to be prophet in your place.
>
> *1 Kings 19:15-16*

To anoint someone is to pour oil on their head to mark them as chosen for a job.
Read the third name again. Elijah is sent to appoint the man who will replace him.
That is a hard kind of mercy: his part has an ending, and he is not the last thing
standing between God and disaster.

Then God corrects the one thing in the complaint that was simply wrong:

> Yet I will leave seven thousand in Israel, all the knees that have not bowed to
> Baal.
>
> *1 Kings 19:18*

Elijah had twice said he alone was left. Seven thousand people he had never met
had refused to worship Baal. He was not the only faithful person in the country,
just the only one he could see.

Paul reached for this passage centuries later, grieving that most of his own
nation had rejected Jesus:

> "Lord... I alone am left." But what is God's reply? "I have kept for myself
> seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal." So too at the present
> time there is a remnant, chosen by grace.
>
> *Romans 11:3-5*

A remnant is the piece left over when most of something is gone. Grace means a
gift given to someone who has not earned it and cannot pay it back. So the seven
thousand were not heroes holding out alone. God kept them.

One honest note, because the seven thousand are not always there to count. Isaiah,
a later prophet, described a servant of God who says this:

> I have labored in vain; I have spent my strength for nothing... yet surely my
> right is with the LORD.
>
> *Isaiah 49:4*

"In vain" means for nothing. This servant is not told his numbers were secretly
good. He holds two things at once: the work looks wasted, and the verdict belongs
to God, not to what he can see. Christians have long read these servant passages
as pointing to Jesus, turned down by most of the people he came to.

## What to do with this

**Eat and sleep before you decide anything.** If you want out of the job, the
church or the friendship, decide nothing until you have had two full nights of
sleep and proper meals.

**Say the true version out loud.** Not the tidy prayer. Tell God the thing you
would be embarrassed to say in front of people. Elijah said "take my life" and was
answered with breakfast.

**Write down two names.** Two people you know who are still going and still
following God. Message one of them this week — the seven thousand made small
enough to see.

**Hand one thing over.** Pick a task you keep because nobody else does it
properly, and give it to someone this month.

Elijah never did die under that bush. Years later he was taken up in a whirlwind
(2 Kings 2:11). Centuries after that, on another mountain, Jesus was praying when
two men appeared beside him:

> Two men were talking with him, Moses and Elijah... and spoke of his departure,
> which he was to accomplish at Jerusalem.
>
> *Luke 9:30-31*

Moses and Elijah — the two men in the Old Testament who told God the job was too
heavy and asked to die. There they are, discussing the death Jesus was going to
accomplish: a word that means finish, get done. Neither of them ever carried the
weight they thought was theirs. It was always his, and he finished it.
