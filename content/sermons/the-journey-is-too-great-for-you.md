There is a kind of tiredness that arrives after the win, not after the loss. You
finish the exam, or the long shift, or the months of caring for someone ill, and
instead of feeling good you sit down and cannot get up.

The Bible tells that story about a man who had just watched God answer him with
fire.

Elijah was a prophet — someone who spoke for God to a nation that did not want to
listen. He lived in Israel about eight hundred years before Jesus, under a king
named Ahab and a queen named Jezebel, who had made the worship of a storm god
called Baal the country's official religion. Elijah challenged that in public. He
gathered the nation on a hill called Carmel, built an altar — a stone table for
burning an offering — and asked God to settle the question in front of everyone.

> Then the fire of the LORD fell and consumed the burnt offering... And when all
> the people saw it, they fell on their faces and said, "The LORD, he is God."
>
> *1 Kings 18:38-39*

A whole crowd changes its mind on the spot. Then Jezebel sends Elijah a message
promising to have him killed, and the story puts no gap between the two scenes. He
runs south, out of the country, into the desert.

## 1. He asked to die right after the biggest day of his life

> He came and sat down under a broom tree. And he asked that he might die, saying,
> "It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life, for I am no better than my
> fathers."
>
> *1 Kings 19:4*

A broom tree is a scrubby desert bush with barely enough shade for one person.
Elijah walks a day past the last town, sits in the only shade there is, and asks
God to end his life. *I am no better than my fathers* means: I thought I would be
the one who finally turned this country around, and I am not.

He never says God failed him. He watched the fire fall. What he cannot carry is
himself — and even at the bottom, he is still talking to God about it.

He was not the first. Moses led Israel out of slavery in Egypt and across a
desert, and partway through he said this:

> I am not able to carry all this people alone; the burden is too heavy for me...
> kill me at once.
>
> *Numbers 11:14-15*

Underneath both men is the same thing: the load is too heavy, and dying looks
easier than carrying it another day. Two of the most important people in Israel's
history said that out loud to God, so it is not proof that a person's faith has
collapsed.

The Bible does not tidy the feeling up. One of Israel's worship songs ends in the
dark and stays there:

> My companions have become darkness.
>
> *Psalm 88:18*

That is the song's last line. Israel kept it for public singing without adding a
happy ending, which tells you how honest God lets his people be with him.

Paul — a travelling teacher who wrote many of the New Testament letters — had his
own version of this, and said what it was for:

> We were so utterly burdened beyond our strength that we despaired of life
> itself... But that was to make us rely not on ourselves but on God who raises
> the dead.
>
> *2 Corinthians 1:8-9*

Paul does not call it pleasant or deserved. He says it took something out of his
hands: he had been carrying the outcome himself, and he stopped. That is Elijah's
trouble under the bush. He thinks a nation's future rests on him, and he has just
found out he cannot lift it.

## 2. God's first answer was food and sleep, given twice

Elijah asks to die, then falls asleep. God's opening move is not a lecture. An
angel — a messenger sent by God — touches him and says, "Arise and eat." There is
flat bread baked on hot stones and a jar of water by his head. He eats and lies
straight back down. So the messenger comes back.

> And the angel of the LORD came again a second time and touched him and said,
> "Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you."
>
> *1 Kings 19:7*

God does not tell Elijah he is stronger than he thinks. God agrees with him. The
journey is too much for you — and that is the reason given for the meal, not a
rebuke. He is fed because it is hard, not told that it is easy.

Jesus later put the same care into an invitation:

> Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my
> yoke upon you... for I am gentle and lowly in heart.
>
> *Matthew 11:28-29*

A yoke is the wooden bar laid across two oxen so they pull a plough together. So
Jesus is not offering an end to work. He is offering a shared harness with himself
on the other side of it. He did the plain version too: when his friends came back
worn out from a hard trip, he took them somewhere quiet to rest (Mark 6:31).

But rest is not an escape hatch, and the Bible will not pretend it is. The night
before he was killed, Jesus prayed in a garden and asked to be spared what was
coming.

> And there appeared to him an angel from heaven, strengthening him. And being in
> agony he prayed more earnestly.
>
> *Luke 22:43-44*

Same pattern — a messenger sent to a man at the end of himself. Now look at what
it did not do. It did not remove what he dreaded and it did not stop the agony. He
was strengthened, and then he went. Elijah eats, then walks forty days deeper into
the desert. Being helped is not the same as being let off.

## 3. God refused to be in the fire

Elijah walks to Horeb, the mountain also called Sinai, where God had given Israel
his laws centuries earlier. God's first words there are a question rather than an
answer: "What are you doing here, Elijah?" Elijah pours out the complaint — the
people have abandoned God, they killed the other prophets, "and I, even I only, am
left, and they seek my life." Then he is sent outside to stand on the mountain.

> And behold, the LORD passed by, and a great and strong wind tore the mountains,
> but the LORD was not in the wind. And after the wind an earthquake, but the LORD
> was not in the earthquake. And after the earthquake a fire, but the LORD was not
> in the fire. And after the fire the sound of a low whisper.
>
> *1 Kings 19:11-12*

Three huge events, each followed by a line saying God was not in it. Remember what
had worked for Elijah days before. Fire. Fire fell and a nation shouted. Now God
sends a fire and stays out of it.

The Hebrew behind that last phrase is *qol demamah daqqah*. *Qol* is a sound or a
voice, *demamah* is silence or stillness, *daqqah* means thin or fine — a sound of
thin silence. English Bibles reach for "a still small voice" or "a low whisper",
and the strange part is in neither. It is *demamah*, the word used for what
happens to a storm once it is over:

> He made the storm be still, and the waves of the sea were hushed.
>
> *Psalm 107:29*

That song is about sailors who thought the sea would kill them, and the word for
the calm they were given is the word standing in Elijah's verse. So what reached
him after the fire was not a whisper telling him what to do next. It was the hush
left when the noise stopped. This mountain had hidden God before: Moses asked to
see him here and was allowed to see only his back (Exodus 33:22-23).

Do not turn that into a rule, though, because the Bible breaks it. Job lost his
children, his health and everything he owned, and demanded an explanation.

> Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind.
>
> *Job 38:1*

Out of the storm, not the calm after it. So "God speaks in the quiet, not the
noise" is not a technique for hearing him. What happened on Horeb is narrower:
God refused to repeat the display that had already worked, because the display was
not what Elijah needed.

Here is the part usually left out. After the wind, the earthquake, the fire and the
thin silence, God asks the same question again — and Elijah gives the identical
answer, word for word. The spectacle changed nothing in him. A powerful experience
is rarely what repairs a person.

> Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke to our fathers by the
> prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son.
>
> *Hebrews 1:1-2*

That was written to Jewish Christians who were close to giving up. Wind,
earthquake, fire, thin silence — each was a piece of the picture. The full and
final word God had to say about himself, he said in a person.

## 4. The cure was a fact, a job, and a partner

God never answers the complaint. He hands out instructions.

> Go, return on your way... you shall anoint Hazael to be king over Syria. And
> Jehu the son of Nimshi you shall anoint to be king over Israel, and Elisha the
> son of Shaphat you shall anoint to be prophet in your place.
>
> *1 Kings 19:15-16*

To anoint someone is to pour oil on their head to mark them as chosen for a job.
Three names, three appointments — and read the third one again. Elijah is sent to
appoint the man who will replace him. That is a hard kind of mercy. His part has
an ending, and he is not the last thing standing between God and disaster.

Then God corrects the one thing in the complaint that was simply wrong:

> Yet I will leave seven thousand in Israel, all the knees that have not bowed to
> Baal.
>
> *1 Kings 19:18*

Elijah had twice said that he alone was left. Seven thousand people he had never
met had refused to worship Baal. He was not the only faithful person in the
country; he was the only one he could see. Despair tells the truth about how you
feel and lies about the facts.

Paul reached for this passage centuries later, grieving that most of his own
nation had not accepted Jesus:

> "Lord... I alone am left." But what is God's reply to him? "I have kept for
> myself seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal." So too at the
> present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace.
>
> *Romans 11:3-5*

A remnant is the piece left over when most of something is gone. Grace means a
gift given to someone who has not earned it and cannot pay it back. Put those
together and Paul's point is sharp: the seven thousand were not self-made heroes
holding out on their own strength. God kept them.

An older book had already named the other thing Elijah was missing — two are
better than one, because if one falls, the other can lift him up
(Ecclesiastes 4:9-10). Elijah had left his servant behind in the last town. The
chapter ends with him finding Elisha ploughing a field and throwing his cloak over
the young man's shoulders (1 Kings 19:19-21).

One honest note, because the seven thousand are not always there to be counted.
Isaiah wrote about a servant of God who says this:

> I have labored in vain; I have spent my strength for nothing and vanity; yet
> surely my right is with the LORD.
>
> *Isaiah 49:4*

"In vain" means for nothing. This servant is not told his numbers were secretly
good. He holds two things at once: the work looks wasted, and the verdict on it
belongs to God rather than to the results he can see. Christians have long read
these servant passages as pointing to Jesus, who was turned down by most of the
people he came to and went to the cross anyway.

## What to do with this

**Eat and sleep before you decide anything.** If you want out — of the job, the
church, the friendship — decide nothing until you have had two full nights of
sleep and proper meals. God fed Elijah before saying a word to him.

**Say the true version out loud.** Not the tidy prayer. Tell God the thing you
would be embarrassed to say in front of people, in the words you would use.
Elijah said "take my life" and was answered with breakfast.

**Write down two names.** Two people you know who are still going, still honest,
still following God. Message one of them this week. That is the seven thousand
made small enough to see.

**Hand one thing over.** Pick a task you keep because nobody else does it
properly, and give it to someone this month. Elijah's recovery included
appointing his own replacement.

Elijah never did die under that bush. Years later he was taken up in a whirlwind
without dying at all (2 Kings 2:11). And there is one more scene. Centuries after
Horeb, on another mountain, Jesus was praying with three of his friends when two
men appeared beside him:

> And behold, two men were talking with him, Moses and Elijah, who appeared in
> glory and spoke of his departure, which he was to accomplish at Jerusalem.
>
> *Luke 9:30-31*

Moses and Elijah. The two men in the Old Testament who told God the job was too
heavy and asked to die. There they are, discussing the death Jesus was going to
accomplish — a word that means finish, complete, get done. Neither of them ever
carried the weight they thought was theirs. It was always his to finish, and he
finished it.
