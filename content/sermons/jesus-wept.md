Two words. It gets quoted as a curiosity, the shortest verse in the Bible, and
usually pressed into service as proof that Jesus was properly human.

Read it in place and it becomes much stranger than that. He is standing outside a
tomb he is about to open. He knows, with a certainty nobody else on that hillside
has, that in a few minutes the man inside will walk out under his own power. And
he cries anyway.

That is the detail worth slowing down for. Not that he wept, but *when*.

## 1. The delay was deliberate

The sisters send word that Lazarus is ill. What John does next is easy to read
past:

> Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. So, when he heard that
> Lazarus was ill, he stayed two days longer in the place where he was.
>
> *John 11:5-6*

Notice the joint. Not *but*. **So.** John connects the love and the waiting with
a therefore, as though the second follows from the first. Every instinct we have
wants that sentence to read "but he stayed," because we treat delay as the
absence of care. The text will not allow it.

Habakkuk was handed the same thing and told to stand in it:

> If it seems slow, wait for it; it will surely come, it will not delay.
>
> *Habakkuk 2:3*

Which sounds like a contradiction until you notice it uses two different
measurements. *Slow* is how it feels from inside the waiting. *Late* is a
judgement about whether it arrived in time. A thing can be genuinely slow and not
one day late, and almost all of our distress lives in the gap where we have
quietly converted the first into the second.

Peter goes further and gives the slowness a direction:

> The Lord is not slow to fulfil his promise as some count slowness, but is
> patient toward you.
>
> *2 Peter 3:9*

The phrase to hold is *as some count slowness*. He does not deny the experience;
he disputes the arithmetic. And the word he reaches for instead of slow is
*patient* — a word about somebody, not a word about a schedule. The delay is
aimed at people.

But we should be honest that this does not always resolve inside the story. Psalm
88 is the one psalm that never turns the corner. It has no closing verse of
confidence. It simply ends:

> Darkness is my closest friend.
>
> *Psalm 88:18*

That is the last line, and the collection let it stand. Some waiting does not get
its explanation on this side, and Scripture declined to edit that out.

## 2. He let them say the hard thing

Martha comes out to meet him on the road, and this is the first thing out of her
mouth:

> Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died.
>
> *John 11:21*

That is an accusation wearing the clothes of faith. She is not asking a question.
She is telling him, to his face, that this is on him. Then Mary arrives and says
the identical sentence, word for word. Both sisters. Neither is corrected. He
does not defend the schedule, though one line about what he is going to do would
have ended the argument. He asks where they have laid him.

The songbook had already made room for this:

> How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face
> from me?
>
> *Psalm 13:1*

That is not a lapse someone slipped past the editors. It is *in the hymnal*,
approved for congregational use. Lament is not the failure of faith; it is a form
of address. You cannot accuse someone you have stopped speaking to.

Job pushes the point somewhere uncomfortable. For thirty-odd chapters his friends
defend God's management with impeccable theology while Job demands a hearing. At
the end, God's verdict goes the way almost nobody expects:

> You have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has.
>
> *Job 42:7*

Said to the friends. The man who complained is the one commended; the men who
explained are the ones rebuked. Whatever God wants from us in grief, tidy
defences of his timing are not obviously it.

And the tension stays sharp, because sometimes the complaint appears to go
nowhere:

> Though I call and cry for help, he shuts out my prayer.
>
> *Lamentations 3:8*

That is in the same chapter as "his mercies are new every morning" — twelve verses
earlier. The book holds both without smoothing either one down, and so should we.

## 3. He wept knowing the ending

Before the two words, John reaches for a verb most translations soften. He says
Jesus was "deeply moved in his spirit and greatly troubled." The Greek is
*enebrimēsato*, from a root used elsewhere of a snort — of scolding, of
indignation. Whatever is happening in him, it is not gentle melancholy. It reads
closer to anger at what death does to people.

And then:

> Jesus wept.
>
> *John 11:35*

He is minutes from the miracle. He knows the ending. Grief is still the correct
response.

Isaiah had sketched this centuries out, and did not describe someone serene:

> A man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief.
>
> *Isaiah 53:3*

*Acquainted* is the word to sit with. Not informed about sorrow, not sympathetic
to it from a distance — on familiar terms with it, the way you know a road you
have walked often.

Hebrews puts the same claim in the present tense and makes it structural:

> We do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathise with our weaknesses.
>
> *Hebrews 4:15*

*Sympathise* there is *sympatheō* — to suffer alongside. Not to observe from a
safe distance and approve of how well you are coping.

Which brings us to the verse most often used to police a grieving person, and what
it actually says:

> That you may not grieve as others do who have no hope.
>
> *1 Thessalonians 4:13*

Read the qualifier. Not *do not grieve*. Not *grieve less*. Grieve differently
from those with nothing to hope for. Paul assumes Christians will weep; he is
describing what the weeping is aimed at, not forbidding it. The one person on that
hillside with complete information about the outcome is the one crying, and no
verse in the New Testament asks him to stop.

## 4. He called him by name

At the tomb he does not issue a general summons:

> Lazarus, come out.
>
> *John 11:43*

A name. The specificity is the point. Whatever else resurrection is, it is not a
category being processed; it arrives addressed to somebody in particular.

That grammar runs back through the whole story. To Moses, on the mountain:

> You have found favour in my sight, and I know you by name.
>
> *Exodus 33:17*

And through Isaiah, to a whole people who had every reason to think they had been
lost in the crowd of empires:

> Fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine.
>
> *Isaiah 43:1*

It runs forward too, all the way to the last book, where the naming becomes so
particular that nobody else can read it:

> A new name written on the stone that no one knows except the one who receives
> it.
>
> *Revelation 2:17*

And then the complication, which the text does not hide. Lazarus walked out of
that tomb and, in time, died again — John even records a plot against his life one
chapter later. This was a reprieve, not a rescue. Paul names the thing that is
still outstanding:

> The last enemy to be destroyed is death.
>
> *1 Corinthians 15:26*

*Last.* Not yet. Bethany was a preview with a date on it, and anyone standing at a
graveside this week is standing on the same side of that verse as Martha was.

## What to do with this

Watch how the chapter ends. The man walks out still wrapped — face bound, hands
bound — and Jesus turns to the people standing there: "Unbind him, and let him
go." Even the raised man needed other hands to get the wrappings off. The miracle
was not the community's job. The unwrapping was, and it still is:

> Bear one another's burdens.
>
> *Galatians 6:2*

So, two directions, depending on where you are standing.

If you are the one grieving: Martha's sentence is available to you. *If you had
been here.* It is not impressive, it is not resolved, and it did not get her
rebuked. Say it, and notice that saying it keeps you in the conversation.

If you are near someone grieving, the instruction is shorter than you would like
and harder than it sounds:

> Weep with those who weep.
>
> *Romans 12:15*

It does not say explain to those who weep. Resist accounting for the delay. You
will want to, because the silence is unbearable and you would like to be useful.
He had better information than you will ever have about how the story ended, and
he did not use it to shorten the weeping. He sat down in it first.

Two words. He wept. Not because he was unsure what came next, but because what
had happened was worth weeping over, and he would not pretend otherwise for the
sake of getting to the good part faster.
