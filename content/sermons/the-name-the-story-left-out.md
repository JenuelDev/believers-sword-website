A woman came back to her home town with nothing. No husband, no sons, no money, no land she could work. Beside her walked a foreign girl who had refused to leave her.

Israel's law had an answer for a family that far gone. Not charity, and not luck. A relative — one particular relative, with a legal duty to pay.

In Ruth chapter 4 that relative turns up. He says yes. Then he hears the full price and says no.

The Bible never tells us his name.

Here is how they got there. In the days before Israel had a king, a famine drove a man called Elimelech from Bethlehem into Moab, a neighbouring country Israel had a long quarrel with. He died there. His two sons married Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth, and then both sons died as well (Ruth 1:1-5). That left his widow Naomi and two foreign daughters-in-law, and in that world a woman's food and safety came through the men of her family.

Naomi went home to Bethlehem and told the younger women to stay in Moab and start again. Orpah did. Ruth would not: wherever you go I will go, she said, your people will be my people and your God my God (Ruth 1:16).

So a foreigner with no claim on the place walks behind the harvesters picking up what they drop (Ruth 2:2-3). Israel's law kept the leftovers of every field for the poor. Legal, and one step above begging. The field she lands in belongs to Boaz, a relative of Naomi's dead husband, and that single fact turns the book.

## 1. In Israel, rescue was a job with a name

Naomi hears whose field it was, and her answer is not "how kind of him".

> The man is a close relative of ours, one of our redeemers.
>
> *Ruth 2:20*

"Redeemer" is a legal word here, not a warm one. The Hebrew is `go'el`: the nearest relative able to act, whose job was to buy back what his family had lost — the land, the freedom, sometimes the person. A duty with a price on it, not a feeling.

The rule sits in Israel's law book.

> If your brother becomes poor and sells part of his property, then his nearest redeemer shall come and redeem what his brother has sold.
>
> *Leviticus 25:25*

Land in Israel was not really for sale. It was the family's share, and if poverty forced a family to let it go, the nearest relative bought it back with his own money. Naomi is not hoping for sympathy; she is remembering that somebody owes her family a rescue.

But the Bible will not let that arrangement look bigger than it is.

> Truly no man can ransom another, or give to God the price of his life, for the ransom of their life is costly and can never suffice.
>
> *Psalm 49:7-8*

To ransom someone is to buy them out. A relative can buy back a field; no relative can buy back a life. The singer looks straight at the death that comes for rich and poor alike and says no family money reaches it. Hold on to that limit.

## 2. The first man said yes until he heard the price

Boaz is a redeemer, but not the nearest one. Another man has the first right to act. Boaz finds him at the town gate, where an Israelite town did its legal business in the open, and calls ten elders over as witnesses (Ruth 4:2).

> And behold, the redeemer of whom Boaz had spoken came by. So Boaz said, "Turn aside, friend; sit down here."
>
> *Ruth 4:1*

"Friend" is the strangest word in the chapter. The Hebrew is `peloni almoni`, and it is not a name. It is a placeholder, the way we say "so-and-so". This writer names everyone else — Elimelech, Naomi, Orpah, Ruth, Boaz, Obed. This one man he will not name.

Boaz opens with the part that sounds like a bargain: Naomi is selling the family field, and this man gets the first offer (Ruth 4:4). He says yes straight away. Of course he does — he hands over money, gets land, and looks generous doing it.

Then Boaz adds the rest. Buying the field also means taking Ruth as a wife, so the dead man's line carries on that land (Ruth 4:5). Israel's law made the first son of such a marriage count legally as the dead man's heir, so the dead man's name would not disappear (Deuteronomy 25:5-6). So the redeemer pays for a field that passes to a boy who is legally somebody else's son.

He hears it and reverses.

> I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I impair my own inheritance. Take my right of redemption yourself, for I cannot redeem it.
>
> *Ruth 4:6*

Read that slowly, because it is not wickedness. It is budgeting. He will not help a widow at a price that damages what he leaves his own children. That is where most careful people land, and why he is uncomfortable to read about.

Jesus met a man who did the same sum and got the same answer. A rich man asked what he must do to have eternal life, and Jesus told him to sell what he owned and come along (Mark 10:21).

> Disheartened by the saying, he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions.
>
> *Mark 10:22*

He is sad, not angry. He wanted it; he simply wanted what he already owned slightly more. That is the ordinary way people turn God down — not with a shout, with a sum.

And Jesus said elsewhere that doing the sum is right.

> For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it?
>
> *Luke 14:28*

So the story leaves a real tension. Counting the cost is wisdom, and the nameless man counted correctly. The law even left him room to refuse (Deuteronomy 25:7-10). Boaz did not skip the arithmetic; he did the same sum, saw the same loss, and paid anyway.

## 3. Boaz paid, and then he took her home

Boaz says it out loud, in front of the elders and everyone standing about.

> You are witnesses this day that I have bought from the hand of Naomi all that belonged to Elimelech... Also Ruth the Moabite, the widow of Mahlon, I have bought to be my wife, to perpetuate the name of the dead in his inheritance.
>
> *Ruth 4:9-10*

Two things happen in one sentence, and they belong together. He pays, and he marries her. He does not send help from a safe distance; he takes the ruined family into his own house and lets his money and his name go to a dead man's family line.

Peter describes a bigger version of the same rescue, in a currency that is not money.

> You were ransomed... not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ.
>
> *1 Peter 1:18-19*

Boaz opened a purse. Remember the limit in Psalm 49: no relative has enough to buy back a life. Peter's answer is that the price was never going to be silver — it was a life for a life, the only currency that reaches that far.

Isaiah had already set both of Boaz's roles, redeemer and husband, beside God's name.

> For your Maker is your husband, the Lord of hosts is his name; and the Holy One of Israel is your Redeemer.
>
> *Isaiah 54:5*

"Hosts" means armies. Isaiah is talking to people who felt abandoned and disgraced, the way a widow felt then, and telling them the commander of armies is the relative who takes their side. Boaz is a small, true picture of something larger: God taking the family duty on himself, because nobody else could afford it.

## 4. He kept his inheritance and lost his name

The book ends with a baby and a list.

> They named him Obed. He was the father of Jesse, the father of David.
>
> *Ruth 4:17*

Obed is Ruth's son, Jesse is Obed's son, David is Jesse's son — the shepherd boy who became Israel's greatest king. So the foreign widow gathering leftovers is the king's great-grandmother.

Now hold that beside a line in Israel's law that is genuinely hard.

> No Ammonite or Moabite may enter the assembly of the Lord. Even to the tenth generation... forever.
>
> *Deuteronomy 23:3*

The assembly of the Lord means Israel gathered as God's people. Ruth is a Moabite, and the book keeps calling her "Ruth the Moabite", including in Boaz's statement at the gate. The same Bible then puts her in the royal family and never stops to explain. Pretending that is simple would be dishonest. Instead of explaining, the book shows you a woman from the wrong nation, with no claim, brought in by a relative who paid.

Matthew opens his account of Jesus with a family list, and keeps her in it.

> And Boaz the father of Obed by Ruth, and Obed the father of Jesse, and Jesse the father of David the king.
>
> *Matthew 1:5-6*

Matthew writes for people who knew that law and would notice that name. He leaves Ruth standing in the line that runs down to Jesus. Another letter says Jesus is not ashamed to call the people he bought his own family (Hebrews 2:11).

Meanwhile the other man is still unnamed. He protected his inheritance exactly as planned, and what he left behind is a blank space in the most famous family tree in the Bible.

## What to do this week

Work out first which person in the story you are. Not Boaz. You are Naomi, home with nothing, or Ruth, with no claim at all. The first move is admitting that and letting someone else pay.

**Name the person you have already priced.** Most of us have worked out what somebody's need would cost and filed them under too expensive — the relative who would need the spare room, the friend whose calls take an hour. The nameless man's mistake was not doing the sum. It was letting the sum have the last word.

**Do one costly thing for them this week.** Not a warm feeling. The lift to the appointment, the call you keep putting off, the money you said you could not spare.

**Stop waiting until you are worth rescuing.** Ruth was gathering leftovers when Boaz paid for her.

The careful man kept everything he was protecting. Nobody remembers what to call him.
