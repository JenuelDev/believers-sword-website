Every building has someone whose name nobody uses. The person who empties the
bins. The one at the checkout. You have walked past them and could not write
their name on an envelope. Genesis 16 is about a person like that.

Abram and Sarai are an old couple with no children, though God has promised Abram
a family too large to count. Later God renames them Abraham and Sarah, which is
how most people know them. Sarai owns a slave — a person owned by someone else,
who cannot leave and does not get asked. Her name is Hagar, and she is Egyptian.

Sarai's plan is that Abram should father a child with Hagar, and the child will
count as Sarai's — an ordinary legal arrangement back then. Nobody asks Hagar.

It works, then it goes wrong. Hagar gets pregnant and looks down on Sarai. Sarai
blames Abram, who hands the problem back — *do to her as you please* — and Sarai
treats her so badly that Hagar runs into the desert while pregnant.

## 1. The first person in the story to say her name is God

Watch what people in the chapter call her. Sarai says "go in to my servant."
Abram says "your servant is in your power." The person telling the story calls
her Hagar. The people in the room never do.

Then she is alone by a spring in the desert, and somebody finds her.

> The angel of the LORD found her by a spring of water in the wilderness... And
> he said, "Hagar, servant of Sarai, where have you come from and where are you
> going?"
>
> *Genesis 16:7-8*

An angel is a messenger — that is all the word means. This is the first time in
the whole Bible that "the angel of the LORD" appears, and he is not sent to
Abram, the man with the promise and the livestock, but to a pregnant runaway. The
first word out of his mouth is her name.

Notice the other word: *found*. She was not praying or looking for God. He went
and found her.

A thousand years later he said the same to Israelites taken as prisoners to a
foreign empire:

> Fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine.
>
> *Isaiah 43:1*

Redeemed means bought back — the word for paying to free someone from slavery.
God tells people at their lowest that he knows exactly who they are. The desert
was not a one-off.

Jesus worked the same way. On the first Easter morning a woman called Mary stood
crying outside his empty grave and thought the man beside her was the gardener:

> Jesus said to her, "Mary." She turned and said to him in Aramaic, "Rabboni!"
> (which means Teacher).
>
> *John 20:16*

One word. Her name. That was all it took — the same move, outside a tomb instead
of beside a spring.

## 2. She only answered half the question

He asked two things: where have you come from, and where are you going.

> I am fleeing from my mistress Sarai.
>
> *Genesis 16:8*

Mistress means the woman who owns her. She answers the first half and says
nothing about the second, because there is nothing to say. The text puts her on
the road to Shur, which runs toward Egypt, the country she was taken out of. She
is not going somewhere. She is going away. A pregnant woman walking into a desert
is not a plan.

Most of us can list what we are running from long before we can name what we are
running to. Away is easy. Toward is hard.

In any case, running from God does not work:

> Where shall I go from your Spirit? Or where shall I flee from your presence?
>
> *Psalm 139:7*

That is from a song by King David. Read coldly it sounds like a threat; in the
song it is the opposite. David lists the furthest places he can imagine and finds
God already there. Hagar could not walk deep enough into that desert to be out of
reach.

But that does not tell you where to go. Jesus answered that part with a story
about a son who took his father's money, ran off, and wasted it:

> But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion,
> and ran and embraced him.
>
> *Luke 15:20*

Slow down on *saw him* — while he was still a long way off. The father had been
watching that road every day. Hagar had no destination. Jesus says there is one,
and someone is watching for you.

## 3. Then he told her to go back

Here is the sentence that stops people, and it should.

> Return to your mistress and submit to her.
>
> *Genesis 16:9*

God sends a mistreated woman back to the household that mistreated her.
Pretending that is easy would be dishonest, so let us not.

Two things this chapter never does: it never says Sarai was right, and it never
praises Abram, who handed a pregnant woman over instead of protecting her.

And the command does not come alone. It is welded to a promise:

> I will surely multiply your offspring so that they cannot be numbered for
> multitude... you shall call his name Ishmael, because the LORD has listened to
> your affliction.
>
> *Genesis 16:10-11*

Catch two things. First the name. *Ishmael* in Hebrew means "God hears", and the
verse says why it was picked. Her son's name is a short sentence about God. Every
time she calls the boy in for a meal, she says out loud that God heard her.

Second, the word affliction. It means being badly hurt by someone with power over
you. God does not call what Sarai did a misunderstanding or a rough patch. He
calls it affliction, and then sends her back into it. Both sit in the same
sentence, and the Bible softens neither.

This is not God's last word on slaves. Centuries later he speaks to a man called
Moses out of a burning bush, about Israelite slaves in Egypt:

> I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt... and I have
> come down to deliver them.
>
> *Exodus 3:7-8*

Same Hebrew word for affliction as in Hagar's story. Same problem, different
answer: this time he brings them out. So "go back" is not God calling slavery
acceptable. It is God, at that point, not finished.

A letter-writer named Paul later put it plainly to a church being pushed around:

> Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God, for it is
> written, "Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord."
>
> *Romans 12:19*

Paul tells them to stop getting their own back. His reason is not that the wrong
was small, but that the case belongs to somebody else who will not drop it. Being
told to stop fighting is not being told nothing happened.

And the honest ending: going back does not fix her life. Years later Hagar is
sent away from that household anyway, with bread and a skin of water (Genesis
21:14). The Bible gives her no tidy finish. It gives her God, twice.

## 4. She gave God a name

Then Hagar does something nobody else in the Bible does.

> So she called the name of the LORD who spoke to her, "You are a God of seeing,"
> for she said, "Truly here I have seen him who looks after me."
>
> *Genesis 16:13*

The name in Hebrew is *El Roi*: God who sees me. Think how odd that is. In these
stories God hands out the names — Abram becomes Abraham, Jacob becomes Israel.
People name altars, wells and children. When Moses asks God who he is, God tells
him. Nobody invents a name for God and has it stick.

Nobody except a foreign slave girl by a spring who had just been properly looked
at for the first time in her life. She names him, and the book keeps it.

She named the water too:

> Therefore the well was called Beer-lahai-roi.
>
> *Genesis 16:14*

That means something like "the well of the Living One who sees me." It went on
the map, and travellers drank there with her sentence about God attached to it.
Isaac — Sarah's son, the child the whole promise was about — later settles at
that well (Genesis 24:62 and 25:11). The son of the house ends up living at water
named by the woman the house drove out.

Her name for God also pointed forward. John opens his book like this:

> And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory.
>
> *John 1:14*

"The Word" is John's way of naming Jesus before he was born as a human. The God
who sees stepped into the world so that he could be seen — walked around, ate
meals, could be pointed at in a crowd.

And when people did look at him, a man called Isaiah had already said what they
would do:

> He was despised and rejected by men... as one from whom men hide their faces.
>
> *Isaiah 53:3*

Isaiah spoke for God hundreds of years before Jesus was killed, describing a
servant of God that people would refuse to look at. The God who found a slave
nobody looked at became a man nobody wanted to look at.

## What to do with this

**Use a name this week.** Find out the name of one person you see often and have
never named — at school, at work, on your street — and say it out loud to them.
It is the first thing God did.

**Answer the second half of the question.** If you are running from something,
write two lines: what I am running from, and what I am running to. If the second
line is blank, that is worth knowing before you start walking.

**Do not call harm something softer than it is.** If someone is hurting you and
you can get out, get out — nothing in Genesis 16 says otherwise. If you cannot
move this week, do what Ishmael's name did and say it once a day: *God heard
that.*

Hagar had no rights, no money, no standing and nobody on her side. She came out
of that desert with a spring, a name for God, and a well still called what she
called it. The chapter is not about how invisible she was. It is about who was
looking.
