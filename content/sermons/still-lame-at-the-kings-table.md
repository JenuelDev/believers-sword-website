A servant knocked on a door in a village out past the Jordan River, a long way from
anywhere important. Inside was a young man who could not walk. When he heard the
king's men had come for him, he had every reason to think he was about to die.

The story sits in the Bible in nine short verses, and it ends with a line most people
read straight past. Good news the whole way, and then this: *he was lame in both his
feet*. Nobody fixes his legs. That is not a loose end. That is the point.

Israel's first king was a man named Saul. He turned against a young soldier named
David, who had become famous for killing an enemy champion, and spent years hunting
him. "Saul sought him every day," the Bible says (*1 Samuel 23:14*).

Saul had a son named Jonathan, and Jonathan did what no prince was supposed to do. He
took David's side, knowing David would one day hold the throne that should have been
his. The two men made a covenant — a binding promise with terms, the kind you cannot
walk away from when it stops suiting you (*1 Samuel 18:3*).

Then Saul and Jonathan were both killed in the same battle, and David became king.

Any reader back then knew what came next: a new king killed the old king's
descendants, because a surviving grandson is a future rebellion. One later king "left
none of them breathing" (*1 Kings 15:29*).

Jonathan's own son had survived. On the day news of that battle arrived, the
five-year-old was picked up by his nurse, who ran with him. She dropped him. He
never walked properly again (*2 Samuel 4:4*).

His name was Mephibosheth. By the time our story
starts he is grown, with a small son of his own, living quietly in somebody else's
house in that far-off village. Nobody has come for him in years.

Then somebody does.

## 1. The king went looking before anyone asked him to

> The king said, "Is there still anyone left of the house of Saul, that I may show him
> kindness for Jonathan's sake?"
>
> *2 Samuel 9:1*

David starts this. Nobody writes him a letter; Mephibosheth does not apply, appeal, or
send a gift. The king has won the palace he fought years for. He is using it to look
for a survivor of his old enemy's family to be good to.

The word behind "kindness" is *hesed*. It does not mean being nice. It means loyal
love that keeps a promise — what you owe because you gave your word, not because the
other person earned it. Two verses later David calls it "the kindness of God"
(*2 Samuel 9:3*).

Centuries later, Jesus described his own work as exactly this kind of search.

> The Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost.
>
> *Luke 19:10*

Jesus said this at the house of a tax collector, a man his neighbours despised for
collecting money for the occupying Romans. "Son of Man" is the title Jesus used for
himself. Notice which verb comes first: *seek*. The search starts before the lost man
knows about it, exactly as David's servants set out before Mephibosheth heard a word.
Nobody cleans himself up first to be looked for.

Paul, writing to Christians in Rome about twenty-five years later, named what that
search cost.

> While we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son.
>
> *Romans 5:10*

"Reconciled" means the fight is over and the relationship repaired. "Enemies" is the
exact word for Mephibosheth: he belonged to the household that had hunted David for
years. And Paul names the bill. David sent servants across the Jordan; God's search
ended at a cross, with the wronged party paying.

Now the honest part, because this same book will not let David be a simple hero. Years
later, during a famine, he hands seven of Saul's other descendants over to be killed.
Mephibosheth is spared only "because of the oath of the LORD that was between them"
(*2 Samuel 21:7*). David's hands are not clean there, and pretending otherwise would be
dishonest. But it makes the next point plain: what kept this man alive was never
anything about this man.

## 2. The welcome was for somebody else's sake

> Do not fear, for I will show you kindness for the sake of your father Jonathan, and I
> will restore to you all the land of Saul your father, and you shall eat at my table
> always.
>
> *2 Samuel 9:7*

David gives him three things: safety, his family's land back, and a permanent seat at
the king's table. Then he says why, and the reason is a dead man. Nothing in the
chapter mentions anything Mephibosheth has done.

The promise behind this was made years earlier, when Mephibosheth was a small child at
most. Jonathan could see his own family was finished. He asked David to keep showing
"the steadfast love of the LORD" to his household forever — that same word, *hesed*
(*1 Samuel 20:14-15*). Mephibosheth is carried into a palace on the strength of an
agreement made before he could understand it.

That should raise an objection, and the Bible raises it first. God's law says a
person's guilt is his own.

> Fathers shall not be put to death because of their children, nor shall children be
> put to death because of their fathers. Each one shall be put to death for his own
> sin.
>
> *Deuteronomy 24:16*

That rule was mercy: it stopped judges from wiping out a criminal's whole household.
But it cuts both ways. If nobody can be punished on someone else's account, how can
anyone be *accepted* on someone else's account?

The debt is not waived. Somebody else pays it in person.

> For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become
> the righteousness of God.
>
> *2 Corinthians 5:21*

Paul is describing a swap. Jesus, who had done nothing wrong, took what belonged to
us. What belonged to him — being right with God — is handed to us.

Sin is answered for, not ignored; it was answered by the one who did not owe it. Being
welcomed for someone else's sake is not a loophole. Somebody paid.

Mephibosheth had already done the arithmetic. "What is your servant," he asks, "that
you should show regard for a dead dog such as I?" (*2 Samuel 9:8*). In that world a dog
was a stray that lived off rubbish. Calling a man one was about the worst insult
available; it is what Goliath threw at David before their fight (*1 Samuel 17:43*).

He is not fishing for reassurance; he has added up what he brings and got nothing.
David does not argue, and does not tell him he is worth more than he thinks. He feeds
him, that night and every night after.

## 3. He came to the table and stayed lame

> So Mephibosheth lived in Jerusalem, for he ate always at the king's table. Now he was
> lame in both his feet.
>
> *2 Samuel 9:13*

The writer had a perfect ending available and refused it. Two verses earlier we are
told Mephibosheth ate "like one of the king's sons" (*2 Samuel 9:11*) — where you stop
for a tidy story. Instead the last thing we hear is the disability. What the king gave
this man was a chair, not a cure.

Jesus once described a dinner party whose guest list matches Mephibosheth exactly. A
man throws a feast, every invited guest makes an excuse, so he sends his servant out
again.

> Go out quickly to the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in the poor and
> crippled and blind and lame.
>
> *Luke 14:21*

Jesus told this at a real meal, to guests competing for the best seats. Read the list
slowly: poor, crippled, blind, lame. Those were the people with no way to repay an
invitation, which is exactly why he names them. And the master never says fix them at
the door.

Paul learned the same thing from the inside. He had a physical trouble he called a
thorn, asked God three times to take it away, and was told no. The answer was "my grace
is sufficient for you" (*2 Corinthians 12:8-9*). Grace means a gift to someone who has
not earned it and cannot pay it back. Paul asked for the problem to leave and was given
the giver instead.

Honesty demands the other side, because the Bible puts it there.

> Jesus said to him, "Get up, take up your bed, and walk." And at once the man was
> healed, and he took up his bed and walked.
>
> *John 5:8-9*

A man who had not walked for thirty-eight years stood up and carried his mat home. So
"God leaves the limp" is no rule either. Sometimes he heals now and sometimes he does
not, and nobody can tell you which. What the Bible never allows is the idea that people
still limping are further from the table.

There is a date for that, just not this one. The last book of the Bible promises a
world where "death shall be no more" (*Revelation 21:4*). Bodies do get mended. That
repair is not the entry requirement.

## What to do with this

**Come to the table before you are fixed.** Do not wait until you have stopped losing
your temper to pray about losing your temper. Pray tonight, in the middle of it, in
ordinary words. The invitation was never sent to a repaired version of you.

**Invite one person who cannot pay you back.** This week, ask someone to a meal who is
unlikely to return the favour: the classmate nobody sits beside, the neighbour who
talks too long, the relative who is hard work. Not a project. Food and a chair.

**When you check your record, answer with the real reason.** The thought comes to most
people: *I have no right to be here*. Arguing about your own worth is the wrong reply,
because it is still a conversation about you. Say the real reason instead — not my
record, his promise.

Mephibosheth limped into that dining room every evening for the rest of his life. Every
servant saw it. Every one of David's own sons saw it. And every evening the food came
anyway, because a king kept a promise to a friend in front of everybody. That room is
what Christians mean by grace.
