Laodicea had money and no water.

The city sat on a plateau in the Lycus valley with no spring worth the name, so
it piped water in from miles off through a stone line whose pipes, where they
have been dug up, are still furred shut with lime. Six miles north, close enough
to see, Hierapolis steamed with hot springs people travelled to sit in. East,
under the mountain, Colossae had cold water off the heights. Laodicea had
neither, and what reached the city arrived flat, chalky and warm.

They were not poor. When an earthquake levelled the place under Nero, Rome
offered relief and Laodicea declined it, rebuilding out of its own treasury. That
is the civic character the last of the seven letters is written to: a town that
could rebuild itself from scratch and could not get a decent drink of water.

The letter opens by naming its author.

> The words of the Amen, the faithful and true witness, the beginning of God's
> creation.
>
> *Revelation 3:14*

*Beginning* is **archē** — origin, source, the point everything starts from, not
the first item in a series. It is the word Paul used of Christ in a letter he
told this town by name to read (Colossians 4:16):

> He is before all things, and in him all things hold together... he is the
> beginning, the firstborn from the dead.
>
> *Colossians 1:17-18*

A generation earlier this congregation was handed a document saying everything
holds together in Christ. Now Christ writes to them himself, and has to introduce
himself again.

## 1. He asks them to be cold

> I know your works: you are neither cold nor hot. Would that you were either
> cold or hot. So, because you are lukewarm... I will spit you out of my mouth.
>
> *Revelation 3:15-16*

The verb is **emeō** — vomit. And in the middle sits a wish the standard sermon
on this text cannot absorb: *would that you were cold.* If cold meant spiritually
dead, Christ has just told a church he would prefer them apostate. Both
temperatures here are good. Hot water is what Hierapolis sold, cold water is what
Colossae had, and each does something for a body. Tepid water only makes you gag.

The charge is not that they lack enthusiasm. It is that they are useless, and
Jeremiah had already put that failure in the medium Laodicea struggled with:

> My people have committed two evils: they have forsaken me, the fountain of
> living waters, and hewed out cisterns for themselves, broken cisterns that can
> hold no water.
>
> *Jeremiah 2:13*

A hewn cistern is an achievement — labour, skill and stone — and worth nothing if
it leaks. The complaint is not that Judah was lazy but that Judah built something
which did not do the one thing it existed for. It still looks like a cistern. You
find out at the bottom of a dry summer.

The honest complication is that the popular reading is not invented out of
nothing. Scripture does prize heat:

> Do not be slothful in zeal, be fervent in spirit, serve the Lord.
>
> *Romans 12:11*

*Fervent* is **zeontes** — boiling. Paul wants the church at a rolling
temperature, so anyone preaching against lukewarmness has a verse to stand on.
The trouble is that Paul offers one good temperature and Jesus offers two. The
cure here cannot simply be more effort, because cold would have been accepted and
cold is not more effort than tepid.

## 2. The inventory was wrong

> For you say, I am rich, I have prospered, and I need nothing, not realising
> that you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind and naked.
>
> *Revelation 3:17*

Three claims, five contradictions. The hinge is *not realising*. They are not
defending this position; they have never examined it. Sin you argue about is at
least in view. These are books nobody has audited in years.

The sentence had been said before, in another kingdom, centuries earlier:

> Ephraim has said, Ah, but I am rich; I have found wealth for myself.
>
> *Hosea 12:8*

Different covenant, different language, same self-assessment. It is what
prosperity says wherever it is comfortable.

Jesus had shown how such an audit ends:

> Soul, you have ample goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry.
> But God said to him, Fool! This night your soul is required of you.
>
> *Luke 12:19-20*

The mechanism is the point. The man is not corrected by a better argument. The
valuation is performed by somebody else, and the figures turn out to have been
wrong for the whole period he was happy with them.

But the charge is not wealth, and Deuteronomy will not let us make it one:

> Beware lest you say in your heart, My power and the might of my hand have
> gotten me this wealth... it is he who gives you power to get wealth.
>
> *Deuteronomy 8:17-18*

Verse eighteen does not confiscate the money; it relocates the credit. Laodicea's
balance was real and God is not against it. What no creature in any tax bracket
can say truthfully is the last clause: *I need nothing.*

## 3. Buy from me

> I counsel you to buy from me gold refined by fire, so that you may be rich, and
> white garments so that you may clothe yourself and the shame of your nakedness
> may not be seen, and salve to anoint your eyes, so that you may see.
>
> *Revelation 3:18*

He has just called them poor and now sends them shopping. The three items are not
random: gold to a banking town, garments to a valley famous for wool that was
dark, eye salve to a district with a medical reputation. Each line hands Laodicea
its own export back, as an import, from someone outside the walls.

The economics are deliberately impossible, and old:

> Come, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters; and he who has no money, come,
> buy and eat! Come, buy wine and milk without money and without price.
>
> *Isaiah 55:1*

That is not a contradiction God failed to notice. It is aimed at the assumption
underneath the word *buy* — that you arrive as a customer with leverage. You
bring nothing and receive everything, but you must come and ask, which is the one
move a self-funded city has trained out of itself.

The goods are not what they look like either. Gold *refined by fire* comes
through the furnace or not at all, so nobody orders it from a catalogue and has
it delivered intact. The garments are handed over already made, which is pointed
in a town that was very good at weaving. Neither can be manufactured locally.

Then the salve, and the hardest sentence attached to it:

> For judgment I came into this world, that those who do not see may see, and
> those who see may become blind... If you were blind, you would have no guilt;
> but now that you say, We see, your guilt remains.
>
> *John 9:39-41*

The ointment is only ever sold to people who will say they cannot see, which is
precisely the sentence Laodicea has announced it does not need. The cure is in
stock and the price is the confession, in a church that has costed itself as
needing nothing.

## 4. He is outside

> Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the
> door, I will come in to him and eat with him, and he with me.
>
> *Revelation 3:20*

This verse usually turns up on evangelistic literature, addressed to someone who
has never believed. Read where it stands, the address is a church — the paragraph
closes with *let him hear what the Spirit says to the churches.* Christ is not
outside the sinner here. He is outside the congregation that meets in his name,
knocking on a door he owns. That is a worse picture than the tract's.

The line before it borrows an older one:

> Those whom I love, I reprove and discipline, so be zealous and repent.
>
> *Revelation 3:19*

> My son, do not despise the Lord's discipline or be weary of his reproof, for
> the Lord reproves him whom he loves.
>
> *Proverbs 3:11-12*

Laodicea is the only one of the seven letters with no commendation in it, and the
only one where he stops to say *whom I love.* The harshness is not the opposite
of the affection; it is the form the affection had to take.

What Christ asks for at the door is dinner. The last time Luke sat him at a table
with people who could not see:

> When he was at table with them, he took the bread and blessed and broke it...
> And their eyes were opened, and they recognised him.
>
> *Luke 24:30-31*

The eye salve and the meal turn out to be one prescription. Sight did not come
from the explanation on the road, though he gave them one for seven miles. It
came at a table.

The complication is the pronoun. *If anyone hears* is singular, sitting inside a
letter to a whole congregation. He does not promise to repair Laodicea; he
promises to come in to whoever opens. So it is possible to eat with Christ in a
church that never answers the door, and possible to sit in that church for years
without hearing the knock.

## The offer at the end

> The one who conquers, I will grant him to sit with me on my throne, as I also
> conquered and sat down with my Father on his throne.
>
> *Revelation 3:21*

That is said to the worst church of the seven, and nothing has been withdrawn.
The mouthful spat out in verse sixteen is offered the throne in verse twenty-one,
and the qualification is not improvement. It is opening a door.

So the week's work is not to generate heat. It is to find the sentence.
Somewhere in your life is something you have stopped praying about, not because
it failed but because it is fine: the marriage that works, the money that covers
it, the job you are good at. That is where *I need nothing* lives, unsaid. Name
one and pray about it this week as though it were in trouble.

Then be quiet long enough to hear a knock. He is at the door at dinner time,
waiting to be asked in by people who own the building.
