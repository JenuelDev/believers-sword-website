Picture a camp in the desert. No walls, no doctor, no town to run to — only tents,
sand, and thousands of people who have walked for years. Then snakes come through the
camp, and people start dying.

The people beg God to take the snakes away. He does not. He tells Moses to make a metal
snake, raise it on a pole, and tell the dying to look at it. It is one of
the strangest rescues in the Bible, and the one Jesus picked to explain what he came to
do.

The setting: Israel had been slaves in Egypt. God brought them out and led them
through the desert toward a land he had promised — a walk of forty years. Moses was the
man out in front, the one God spoke through. They were taking the long way round,
because the king of Edom would not let them cross his country (*Numbers 20:21*).

> "Why have you brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? For there is no
> food and no water, and we loathe this worthless food." Then the LORD sent fiery
> serpents among the people, and they bit the people, so that many people of Israel
> died.
>
> *Numbers 21:5-6*

Read the complaint slowly: it argues with itself. There is no food — and we hate the
food. God had been feeding them every morning with bread they never planted,
and they called it worthless.

Then the hard sentence. The text does not say the snakes wandered in; it says God sent
them. Speaking against God is not a small thing.

Now comes the part nobody expects.

## 1. They asked God to take the snakes away, and he left them there

> The people came to Moses and said, "We have sinned... Pray to the LORD, that he take
> away the serpents from us." So Moses prayed for the people.
>
> *Numbers 21:7*

This is a real change of heart. They admit what they did and ask for one thing: get rid
of the snakes. Anyone would pray it: remove the problem, put things back the
way they were. Moses prays, and God does not do what they asked. The snakes stay. He
gives them a way to live through them.

The same pattern shows up in Psalm 107, an old song about people who fell ill through
their own foolishness.

> Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble, and he delivered them from their
> distress. He sent out his word and healed them.
>
> *Psalm 107:19-20*

Notice how rescue arrives. God does not tidy the situation away — he *sends* something,
and it has to be received. Help comes from outside.

Jesus said something close to that on his last night before he was killed, to friends
who were frightened.

> In the world you will have tribulation. But take heart; I have overcome the world.
>
> *John 16:33*

"Tribulation" is an old word for hard pressure. His friends wanted a promise that
nothing bad would happen, and he would not give it. Trouble is certain, he said, and he
is bigger than it. That is the shape of the answer at the pole: the snakes stay, and God
is bigger than the snakes.

## 2. The cure was shaped like the thing that was killing them

> And the LORD said to Moses, "Make a fiery serpent and set it on a pole, and everyone
> who is bitten, when he sees it, shall live."
>
> *Numbers 21:8*

Look at what God asks for. Not a medicine. Not a wall. A model of the exact creature
killing people, raised where nobody can miss it. The dying had to stare at the shape of
the thing that bit them.

Jesus explained that pole himself, to a religious teacher named Nicodemus. The man came
at night so nobody would see him asking questions.

> As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted
> up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life.
>
> *John 3:14-15*

"Son of Man" was the title Jesus used for himself, and "eternal life" means life with
God that death does not end. "Lifted up" does two jobs here. It means raised high in
honour. It also means raised on a cross — the wooden frame the Romans, who ruled the
country then, used for public executions. The snake went up so people could live; he
goes up for the same reason.

The cure still takes the shape of the problem. Paul, an early Christian teacher,
explains why. He quotes an old law of Israel — "a hanged man is cursed by God"
(*Deuteronomy 21:23*) — then writes:

> Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us — for it is
> written, "Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree."
>
> *Galatians 3:13*

To redeem someone is to buy them out of debt or slavery, at a price. Christ — Paul's
word for Jesus — did not rescue anyone from a safe distance. He went to the cursed
place and took the curse.
The bronze snake could not bite, and Jesus had done nothing wrong. Both were hung up,
looking like the trouble they cured. What was killing you landed on him instead.

## 3. The instruction was not "fight the snakes." It was "look."

> And if a serpent bit anyone, he would look at the bronze serpent and live.
>
> *Numbers 21:9*

Notice what God did not ask for. No hunting parties. No proving you were sorry enough.
A bitten man could do none of it. He was on the ground. He could turn his head. That
was the whole requirement.

Paul, writing to a church in the city of Ephesus, names why God set it up this way.

> For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is
> the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast.
>
> *Ephesians 2:8-9*

Grace means a gift handed to someone who has not earned it and cannot repay it. The
reason for the design is that last phrase: *so that no one may boast*. Nobody in that
camp survived by being faster or braver. Looking is what you do when you cannot do
anything — a child with a splinter must hold still and let someone else work.

But do not turn that into "nothing happens afterwards".

> Let us run with endurance the race that is set before us, looking to Jesus, the
> founder and perfecter of our faith.
>
> *Hebrews 12:1-2*

Here looking is something you keep doing while you run, not one glance you file away.
The writer is speaking to Christians who were tired and ready to quit. The look that
saves a man in a second is the one he holds for a lifetime.

## 4. The cure became an idol, and a good king smashed it

Moses obeyed, and the rescue worked. It also left an object behind.

> So Moses made a bronze serpent and set it on a pole.
>
> *Numbers 21:9*

Bronze does not rot. When the last bite healed, the pole was still standing, and Israel
kept it. That is where the story turns in a way most people never hear.

Long after Moses, a king of Judah — the southern half of Israel — named Hezekiah went
through the country pulling down everything his people wrongly worshipped. One item is
a shock.

> And he broke in pieces the bronze serpent that Moses had made, for until those days
> the people of Israel had made offerings to it (it was called Nehushtan).
>
> *2 Kings 18:4*

For centuries they had burned offerings to Moses' pole. The object God gave to point
them to himself had become the thing itself, a lucky charm on a stick. The name in that
verse is built from the ordinary Hebrew words for bronze and snake. It was only shaped
metal, and Hezekiah broke it up.

That is uncomfortable, because God told Moses to make it. It sits oddly
beside one of the ten commands God gave the same nation at Mount Sinai.

> You shall not make for yourself a carved image, or any likeness of anything that is
> in heaven above, or that is in the earth beneath.
>
> *Exodus 20:4*

God can give his people something to look at without meaning it to be worshipped, and
given time they will worship it anyway. An idol is not only a statue
of a false god. It is any good thing you treat as the source.

So the last word is a person, not an object.

> "And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself." He said
> this to show by what kind of death he was going to die.
>
> *John 12:32-33*

John stops his story to explain the phrase: lifted up means the cross. The snake drew
eyes to a lump of bronze; Jesus draws people *to himself*. You cannot keep him in a
cupboard and burn offerings to him. He is alive.

## What to do with this

**When the thing will not go away, ask the second question.** Keep asking God to take it
away; the camp was right to ask. Then add the question they had to learn: *if this
stays, what have you given me to look at while it does?* Pray that tonight about the
trouble you have prayed about ten times.

**Say the true thing before you feel any better.** The bitten man did not wait for the
swelling to go down before looking up. If you have done something wrong, name it
today, out loud, in ordinary words. Do not wait until you can bring God a tidy version
of yourself.

**Check what you have turned into a charm.** The Bible on your shelf, the seat you
always sit in, the routine you would feel unsafe skipping — good things, which is
exactly how this goes wrong. Ask of each: if I lost it tomorrow, would I still have
Christ? If the answer is no, you have found your Nehushtan.

Nobody in that camp was healed for being sorry, or strong, or quick. They were
healed because they turned their heads toward something God had
lifted up where they could see it. Every one of them was still lying in a camp full of
snakes.
