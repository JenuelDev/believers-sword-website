Imagine being handed exactly one day's food and told to keep none of it back. Not
most of it. None.

Nothing at the back of the cupboard for a bad week. Anything you did hide would be
spoiled and crawling with worms by morning. Every day you would have to go outside
and look again.

That is how God fed a nation for forty years, and the spoiling was not a flaw. He
built it in.

The story sits in Exodus, the second book of the Bible. Israel, a family grown
into a nation, had spent generations as slaves in Egypt, doing forced building
work for a king who owned them. God brought them out. A month later they were crossing desert, with no farms
and nothing to buy.

Hungry people say what they think. This is what they said to Moses and Aaron, the
brothers leading them:

> Would that we had died by the hand of the LORD in the land of Egypt, when we sat
> by the meat pots and ate bread to the full, for you have brought us out into
> this wilderness to kill this whole assembly with hunger.
>
> *Exodus 16:3*

They would rather be dead slaves with full stomachs than free people who are
hungry. Notice how fast memory sweetened Egypt. *Meat pots*, as though slaves ate
well. Hunger makes the old cage look like a kitchen.

God does not tell them off. He feeds them — but in a way that removes the thing
they wanted most, a supply they could count.

## 1. He gave them food nobody could name

The next morning, after the dew dried, something lay on the ground.

> There was on the face of the wilderness a fine, flake-like thing, fine as frost
> on the ground. When the people of Israel saw it, they said to one another, "What
> is it?"
>
> *Exodus 16:14-15*

They found breakfast and could not say what it was. The Hebrew behind "What is
it?" is *man hu*, and the question became the name — Exodus 16:31 says they called
it *manna*. Food you can name is food you can plan around. This fitted nothing
they already knew.

Moses gave the reason forty years later:

> He humbled you and let you hunger and fed you with manna, which you did not
> know … that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone, but
> man lives by every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD.
>
> *Deuteronomy 8:3*

Read the order. God let them go hungry first, then fed them; the hunger was part
of the lesson, not a delay before it. The point was never that food falls from the
sky. It was that a life is held up by God speaking, not by what is in the cupboard.

Centuries later a Jewish teacher named Jesus walked into a desert of his own.
After forty days without food, someone told him to turn the stones into bread. He
answered with that line:

> Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth
> of God.
>
> *Matthew 4:4*

Israel complained in the wilderness when food ran out. Jesus was in a wilderness,
starving, able to make bread on the spot, and would not. He is not quoting an old
verse. He is the first person in the story to live it.

## 2. It came one day at a time, and it would not keep

God set the rule before the first flake fell. They were to gather one day's
portion each day, "that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law or not"
(Exodus 16:4). The test is not whether they can bear hunger. It is whether they
will take today's amount and stop.

Some did not.

> Moses said to them, "Let no one leave any of it over till the morning." But they
> did not listen to Moses. Some left part of it till the morning, and it bred worms
> and stank.
>
> *Exodus 16:19-20*

Somebody kept a portion back, and who would not. Saving food is sensible, which is
what makes this hard — the instinct being blocked is a good one. They woke to a
stinking, crawling jar. A private store would have quietly replaced the God who
sent it.

Jesus later taught a prayer built on the same measure:

> Give us this day our daily bread.
>
> *Matthew 6:11*

A small request, on purpose — a day's worth, asked for today, which means coming
back tomorrow.

A short prayer in Proverbs, a book of brief sayings about how life works, asks for
the same thing and names the danger on both sides:

> Give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with the food that is needful for
> me, lest I be full and deny you and say, "Who is the LORD?" or lest I be poor
> and steal and profane the name of my God.
>
> *Proverbs 30:8-9*

He wants enough, and gives his reasons. Too much and he will forget God, because
people who need nothing rarely ask. Too little and he will steal. Most of us pray
about one of those dangers, never the first.

Then the passage bends its own rule. On the sixth day they gathered double, and it
kept overnight, because the seventh day was a rest day and nothing fell (Exodus
16:22-26). Same food, one day later, no worms. Storing was
never the sin. The rule was about doing what the giver said.

## 3. Nobody ended up with more than anybody else

Then they measured what they had gathered.

> When they measured it with an omer, whoever gathered much had nothing left over,
> and whoever gathered little had no lack. Each of them gathered as much as he
> could eat.
>
> *Exodus 16:18*

An omer was a set measuring container, and the passage treats one omer as one
person's food for a day. Fast and slow gatherers ended the morning with the same
amount each. Nobody came out of that field ahead.

A travelling teacher named Paul reached for this verse a thousand years later,
while collecting money for poorer Christians far away:

> Your abundance at the present time should supply their need … that there may be
> fairness. As it is written, "Whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and
> whoever gathered little had no lack."
>
> *2 Corinthians 8:14-15*

Paul applies a rule about desert bread to money. The leftover in your hand was
never meant to sit there; it is somebody else's missing portion, in the wrong
place. That is harder than most talk about giving.

Now the honest part. Years in, the same nation looked at the same miracle and
hated it:

> We remember the fish we ate in Egypt that cost nothing, the cucumbers, the
> melons, the leeks, the onions, and the garlic. But now our strength is dried up,
> and there is nothing at all but this manna to look at.
>
> *Numbers 11:5-6*

Bread from heaven, described as *nothing at all*. The fish in Egypt did not cost
nothing; they paid with their lives as slaves. But they are not entirely wrong,
and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Enough is not the same as exciting,
and God often sends the same plain thing again today.

## 4. The bread stopped, because it was never the real bread

The chapter ends with a number.

> The people of Israel ate the manna forty years, till they came to a habitable
> land.
>
> *Exodus 16:35*

Forty years, then it stops. Children grew up on it and knew no other food. But it
had an end date: the day after Israel first ate food grown in their own land, the
manna ceased for good (Joshua 5:12). It had been bread for the road, not the
destination.

Much later a crowd chasing Jesus round a lake brought the manna up themselves.
They wanted another free meal, so they reminded him what Moses managed: *our
fathers ate the manna in the wilderness* (John 6:31). Jesus turned it around.

> Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died. This is the bread
> that comes down from heaven, so that one may eat of it and not die.
>
> *John 6:49-50*

He says it plainly: everyone who ate that miracle food is dead now. It held them
one day at a time and then stopped, as bread does. Something that pushes death
back until tomorrow is not an answer to death.

> I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever
> believes in me shall never thirst.
>
> *John 6:35*

He is claiming to be what the desert food was a picture of — a supply that does
not run out, given rather than earned, taken by coming to him and trusting him.
Forty years of flakes on the ground had been a slow lesson in how to receive.

The crowd did not enjoy it. Many of those following Jesus turned back that day and
stopped walking with him (John 6:66). They wanted a bread king. They got a man
saying he was the bread, and they left. It was never an easy claim.

## What to do with this

**Ask for today before you plan the year.** Tonight, name one thing you have that
you did not make — a meal, a bed, someone who picked up the phone — and thank God
for it. Keep it that small.

**Find the portion in your hand that belongs to somebody else.** Look at what you
have spare this month, not what you would have spare in a better year. Pick one
person with less and move some of it to them before the month ends.

**When the provision is boring, say so, and eat it anyway.** The same meal, the
same job, the same slow week is still the bread. Notice the complaint, name it,
and go back out to the field in the morning.

For forty years those people woke and looked at the ground to see whether God had
shown up again. He had, every time, and they still could not save any of it. That
was the training. Then the bread ran out, as bread does, and a man stood in front
of a hungry crowd and said the supply was standing there talking to them.
